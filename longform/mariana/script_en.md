# Mariana Trench — script v1 (English)

Format: `N:` narrator · `TRAVELLER:` our time-traveller mascot · `SNAILFISH:` / `AMPHIPOD:` cartoon deep-sea skit characters. `*word*` = keyword caption. `[tag]` = delivery. Target ~1,700 words ≈ 10.5 min.
Real people (Piccard, Walsh, Cameron, Vescovo) appear only in real photos. Scientific estimates are framed as estimates.
Fact sources: see sources.md.

---

# HOOK

N: In 2019, a man sank to the deepest point on Earth. Almost *eleven kilometres* straight down, into total darkness.
N: Only a handful of humans had ever been here. He switched on his lights, looked out the window... and saw something waiting for him on the sea floor.
N: A *plastic bag*.
TRAVELLER: [stunned, lowering his pocket watch] Eleven kilometres down... and we still got there first?
N: But that bag is not the strangest thing hiding in the Mariana Trench. Not even close.
N: Down here, the water can crush steel. Fish glow like lanterns. Shrimp-like creatures grow as big as your hand. And under the trench floor, something is slowly *swallowing the ocean*.
N: So today, we're going all the way down. Metre by metre. Until we hit the bottom of the world.
TRAVELLER: [tightening his goggles, nervous] I'm going to need a bigger submarine.

# CHAPTER 1: THE HOLE AT THE BOTTOM OF THE WORLD

N: First, where is it?
N: Look at the western Pacific. South of Japan, east of the Philippines, near the island of *Guam*. There's a long, curved scar in the ocean floor, shaped like a crescent moon.
N: It's about *two and a half thousand kilometres* long. That's longer than a flight from Delhi all the way to Chennai.
N: And at its southern end is the deepest spot ever measured on our planet: the *Challenger Deep*. Nearly *eleven kilometres* below the waves.
N: To feel how deep that is, take the tallest mountain on Earth. *Mount Everest*. Pick it up... and drop it into the trench.
TRAVELLER: [squinting down] Where did it go?
N: Exactly. Even the peak of Everest would still be under about *two kilometres* of water.
N: So how does a hole this big even exist?
N: The Earth's surface is broken into giant plates, and they're always moving. Here, the huge *Pacific Plate* runs into a smaller one, the *Mariana Plate*... and slides underneath it, diving down into the Earth.
N: Where one plate bends and sinks below the other, the sea floor gets pulled down into a deep, narrow valley. That valley is the trench.
TRAVELLER: [impressed] So the ocean floor is... sinking. On purpose.
N: Very slowly. Just a few centimetres a year. About as fast as your fingernails grow. Remember that. It's going to matter later.

# CHAPTER 2: WHERE THE LIGHT DIES

N: Let's start the dive.
N: At the surface it's bright, warm, and full of life. But sunlight fades fast in water.
N: By *two hundred metres*, it's like dusk. Most of the colours are gone. Red disappears first, then yellow, then green... until only a deep blue is left.
N: By *one thousand metres*, the sunlight is gone. *Completely.* From here to the bottom, it's the darkest place you can imagine. Darker than any night on land.
TRAVELLER: [whispering] Can somebody switch on a light?
N: Someone already has. Down here, animals make their own light. It's called *bioluminescence*.
N: Jellyfish flash blue when they're touched. Some shrimp spit glowing clouds to escape. And the anglerfish carries a little glowing lure on its head, like a fishing rod... and waits for something curious to swim close.
SNAILFISH: [cheerful, from the dark] Ooh. Nice light. Very friendly-looking.
TRAVELLER: [panicking] Don't go towards the light! Never go towards the light!
N: And we're only one kilometre down. The trench goes ten more.

# CHAPTER 3: THE WEIGHT OF THE OCEAN

N: Now here's the real monster of the deep. And it isn't an animal.
N: It's *pressure*.
N: Right now, at the surface, the air is pressing on you with a weight called one *atmosphere*. You don't feel it because you were born in it.
N: But water is much heavier than air. Every ten metres you go down, you add one more atmosphere.
N: At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, that adds up to more than *one thousand atmospheres*.
N: That's like having more than *seven tonnes* pressing on every square inch of your body. Think of an elephant... standing on your thumbnail.
TRAVELLER: [wincing, hiding his thumb] Please don't say elephant.
N: Pressure like that crushes air spaces flat. Lungs, empty bottles, ordinary submarines. A normal military submarine would be crushed long before it got anywhere near the bottom.
N: So to get a human down there alive, you need a very special machine. And in 1960, two men climbed into one.

# CHAPTER 4: THE FIRST TWO MEN

N: Its name was the *Trieste*.
N: It didn't look like a submarine. It looked like a giant metal balloon, because that's basically what it was. A huge float filled with petrol, which is lighter than water, and hanging underneath it, a small steel ball for the crew.
N: Inside that ball were two men: Swiss engineer *Jacques Piccard* and U.S. Navy officer *Don Walsh*.
N: On the twenty-third of January, *1960*, they began sinking into the Challenger Deep.
N: The descent took almost *five hours*. It was cold. It was cramped. And at around nine kilometres down...
N: ...there was a loud *bang*. The whole sphere shook.
TRAVELLER: [frozen] That's... not a good sound.
N: One of the outer windows had *cracked*. With a thousand atmospheres waiting outside.
N: They checked their instruments. The sphere was holding. So they made a decision that still sounds crazy today. They *kept going*.
N: And they touched the bottom. The first humans ever to reach the deepest place on Earth.
N: They stayed for about *twenty minutes*. The landing had stirred up a cloud of fine mud, so they could barely see. Then they started the long ride back up.
TRAVELLER: [shaking his head, smiling] Deepest picnic in history.
N: Then... nobody went back. For *fifty-two years*.
N: In *2012*, film director *James Cameron*, the man who made *Titanic*, dived down alone in a narrow green sub called the *Deepsea Challenger*. Since then, a few more explorers have made it, including *Victor Vescovo*, the man who found that plastic bag.
N: Think about that. *Twelve* people have walked on the Moon. Until 2019, only *three* had ever been to the Challenger Deep.
N: And when they got there, they found something nobody expected.

# CHAPTER 5: SOMETHING IS ALIVE DOWN HERE

N: *Life.*
N: No sunlight. Freezing water. Crushing pressure. And still... there are animals living at the bottom of the trench.
N: Like the *snailfish*. A small, pale, almost see-through fish with a soft, jelly-like body.
SNAILFISH: [proud, wiggling] Soft is the secret. No bones to crack, no air to squeeze.
N: Its body is made for the pressure. Its bones are soft, it has no swim bladder full of air, and special molecules in its cells help stop the pressure from bending its proteins out of shape.
N: In 2022, scientists filmed a snailfish swimming at *eight thousand three hundred and thirty-six metres*, in a trench just north of the Mariana, off Japan. The deepest fish ever caught on camera.
N: Below that depth, scientists think fish can't survive. But other animals can.
AMPHIPOD: [booming, comically huge] Did somebody say *shrimp*?
N: These are *amphipods*. Relatives of the tiny sand-hoppers you find on a beach. But in the deep trenches, some species grow far bigger than normal. Sometimes as long as your hand. Scientists call it *deep-sea gigantism*.
TRAVELLER: [backing away slowly] I liked them better small.
N: They crawl across the sea floor eating anything that sinks from above. Dead fish. Old leaves. Even scraps of wood.
N: And there are stranger things. Giant single-celled creatures called *xenophyophores*, each one just *one cell*, but as big as a lemon, have been found more than ten kilometres down in this trench.
N: Life, it turns out, will move in anywhere. Even here.
N: But the biggest secret of the Mariana Trench isn't living on the sea floor.
N: It's happening *underneath* it.

# CHAPTER 6: THE TRENCH THAT DRINKS THE OCEAN

N: Remember the Pacific Plate, sliding down under the Mariana Plate a few centimetres a year?
N: It doesn't go down dry.
N: As the plate bends into the trench, it cracks. Seawater seeps into those cracks and soaks into the rock. And then that wet rock is carried *down* with the plate, deep into the Earth.
TRAVELLER: [horrified, looking at the sea] Wait. The ocean is... leaking?
N: In a way, yes. And in 2018, scientists found it's leaking much more than anyone thought.
N: A team from Washington University in St. Louis placed *nineteen* earthquake sensors on the floor around the trench and listened to the Earth shake for more than a year.
N: From those vibrations, they could tell how deep the wet rock goes. Their answer: almost *thirty kilometres* below the sea floor.
N: They estimated that around the Mariana Trench, about *four times* more water is being dragged into the Earth than earlier calculations said. And worldwide, through all these trenches, maybe *three times* more.
TRAVELLER: [checking his pocket watch, worried] So when does the ocean run out?
N: Here's the twist. It doesn't. Sea levels have stayed fairly steady for millions of years. So if all this water is going down... some of it must be coming back up. Through volcanoes. Through vents.
N: And right next to the trench, we can actually *see* that.
N: On an underwater volcano called *NW Eifuku*, about one and a half kilometres down, scientists found a vent they named *Champagne*.
N: Because bubbles were streaming out of the rock. But they weren't air. They were droplets of *liquid carbon dioxide*. Squeezed so hard by the pressure that the gas had turned into a liquid.
TRAVELLER: [delighted] Underwater champagne! Finally, a party!
N: Not quite. The water around it is so acidic that shellfish there struggle to build their shells.
N: So the Mariana Trench is a place where the ocean goes *in*, and where the planet breathes *out*. One of the places where our world recycles itself.
N: Which makes what we've been throwing into it... a bigger problem.

# CHAPTER 7: WE GOT THERE FIRST

N: Let's go back to that plastic bag.
N: It wasn't alone. On those dives, explorers also spotted *candy wrappers* near the bottom.
N: And in 2019, scientists led by deep-sea biologist *Alan Jamieson* went looking inside those amphipods from the deepest trenches on Earth, including the Mariana.
N: They found tiny pieces of *plastic* and synthetic fibres in their guts. In the Mariana Trench samples... *every single one* they checked had eaten them.
AMPHIPOD: [sad, small voice now] I thought it was dinner.
TRAVELLER: [quiet, taking off his goggles] Eleven kilometres down. In a place almost no human has seen.
N: Plastic sinks slowly. It drifts. It breaks into smaller and smaller pieces. And the trench, like a giant funnel, collects what falls.
N: The deepest place on our planet. And it already has our fingerprints on it.

# ENDING

N: So that's the Mariana Trench.
N: A crack in the Earth so deep it could swallow Everest. A place where light dies, where a single window crack could have ended the first dive, where fish glow and shrimp grow giant.
N: A place that, very slowly, is drinking the ocean... and breathing it back out.
N: And still, we've explored only a tiny part of it. Most of the deep sea floor has never been seen up close by human eyes. Not once.
TRAVELLER: [winding his pocket watch, grinning] So the bottom of the world... still has secrets.
N: It does. And we'll keep going after them.
N: If you want to see where we dive next, subscribe to *BHUGOL*. And tell us in the comments: would *you* get into that little steel ball and go down?
TRAVELLER: [saluting] See you at the bottom.

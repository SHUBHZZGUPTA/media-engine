# India remake: script (English), written to the Long-form History Script Playbook

Format: `N:` narrator · scene speakers in CAPS (real historical people + one in-scene bystander) · `[SKIT: …]` scene description · `*word*` = keyword caption · `[tag]` = delivery. No commentator lines.
Numbers marked ‡ are historians' estimates (Maddison) and are framed as estimates on screen.

---

# HOOK

N: May, fourteen ninety-eight. A Portuguese ship drops anchor off *Calicut*, on India's southwest coast. The captain sends one man ashore first: a convict, in case things go wrong. Two North African merchants spot him.
[SKIT: Calicut market 1498, a sunburnt Portuguese convict in a torn shirt, two North African merchants in turbans beside sacks of pepper]
MERCHANT: [shocked] May the Devil take you! What brought you here?
CONVICT: [out of breath] We came looking for Christians... and spices.
N: That's in the ship's own journal. All around him: sacks of *black pepper*, and painted *cotton cloth* so fine that Europe would name it after this very port. *Calico*.
N: Back then, India made about *a quarter* of everything the whole world produced.‡
N: That wealth pulled everyone in. An emperor would apologise for a war, *in stone*. A diamond would travel from a *peacock throne* to the *Tower of London*. And a trading company would raise an army *bigger than Britain's*.
N: The numbers on your phone were first written here. Even the English word *loot* is Hindi.
N: By nineteen fifty, India's share of the world was about *four percent*.‡ So how did the richest shop on Earth get robbed again and again... and why did almost every robber *end up staying*?
N: The answer starts in a city with *better drains* than many cities today... and a writing system *nobody on Earth can read*.

# CHAPTER 1: THE CITY UNDER THE RAILWAY

N: In the eighteen-fifties, British engineers were building a railway across Punjab, from *Lahore to Multan*. They had a problem. A railway needs millions of bricks, and Punjab had plenty of mud and very few stones.
N: Then, near a village called *Harappa*, they found a hill made of bricks. Old, hard, perfect bricks, just lying in the ground.
[SKIT: 1850s Punjab railway camp, a British engineer in a pith helmet, a local foreman pointing at a mound of old bricks]
FOREMAN: Sir, there's an old ruin over there. Full of bricks.
ENGINEER: [delighted] Free bricks? Splendid. Old ruins never matter.
FOREMAN: Sir, there are little stone stamps in here too. With animals on them.
ENGINEER: Keep the stamps. Ship the bricks.
N: According to the archaeologist *Alexander Cunningham*, Harappa's bricks were enough to lay about *a hundred miles* of that railway. Keep those bricks in mind. They'll be back.
N: Only in the nineteen-twenties did archaeologists realise what had gone under the tracks: one of the *oldest civilisations on Earth*.
N: About forty-five hundred years ago, while Egypt was building its pyramids, cities were growing along the *Indus River*, in today's *Pakistan and northwest India*. Together they covered more land than Egypt and Mesopotamia *combined*.
N: And they were weirdly *modern*. Straight streets. Bricks of the *same size* in every city, for hundreds of years. Many houses had their own *bathroom*, with wastewater running into *covered drains* under the street.
N: Four and a half thousand years ago. Some cities today are still working on that.
N: In the middle of *Mohenjo-daro* sat a brick pool, sealed with tar so it wouldn't leak: the *Great Bath*. Nobody knows what it was for. Maybe ceremonies. Maybe a very fancy swimming pool.
N: And the people here were doing something that would shape this land's fortune for the next four thousand years. They were growing *cotton*, and weaving it. Some of the earliest cotton cloth ever found comes from this region. Remember that cloth. It's going to be worth more than gold.
N: They traded far, too. Their seals turn up in *Mesopotamia*, in today's Iraq. And on those seals, they wrote. *Hundreds* of different signs.
N: Nobody can read them. Not one word. To this day.
[SKIT: museum study, a grey-haired scholar with a magnifying glass, a student, a small stone seal with a unicorn]
STUDENT: What does it say?
SCHOLAR: [squinting] Fish. Jar. Unicorn.
STUDENT: So... a king's name?
SCHOLAR: Or a grocery list. We genuinely don't know.
N: We don't know their kings, their gods, or what they called themselves. Oddly, nobody has found grand palaces or giant royal tombs either. Just well-planned cities, built for ordinary people.
N: Then, around nineteen hundred BCE, the great cities slowly emptied. Rivers shifted course. One may have dried up. The monsoon grew weaker. People drifted into villages, and the drains filled with mud.
N: So here's the first answer to our question. This land's first great treasure wasn't stolen at all. Nature took it back.
N: But over the mountains in the northwest, new people were coming. And they carried something they would guard more carefully than gold: hymns so sacred that, for more than *three thousand years*, nobody was allowed to write them down.

# CHAPTER 2: THE PRINCE WHO WASN'T ALLOWED TO SEE SADNESS

N: The culture that grew up in north India is known by its sacred hymns: the *Vedas*. The oldest, the *Rigveda*, is more than three thousand years old.
N: Where its composers came from is one of the most argued-about questions in Indian history. Many historians, using clues from languages and ancient DNA, think speakers of an Indo-Aryan language arrived gradually from Central Asia and mixed with people already here. Others argue the culture grew mostly from within India. The debate is very much alive.
N: What nobody argues about is this. The Vedas were *memorised*, sound for sound. Priests trained from childhood to chant them forwards, backwards, in pairs and in triplets, so not a single syllable would change. UNESCO now lists that chanting as part of humanity's heritage.
[SKIT: forest school, an old guru with a long beard, a tired young student]
GURU: Again. From the beginning. Backwards this time.
STUDENT: Guruji, we've done this hymn forty times.
GURU: And tomorrow we do it forty more.
STUDENT: Can't we just write it down?
GURU: [offended] Write it down? And trust a *leaf*?
N: Over the centuries, these communities moved east into the great plain of the *Ganga*. They cleared forests, farmed rice, learned iron, and built kingdoms. By about six hundred BCE, north India had *sixteen* big states.
N: Society was hardening too, into ranked groups of priests, warriors, traders and workers. Over many centuries, that system, *varna*, would set into the rigid rules of caste.
N: And then, around two and a half thousand years ago, a king in the foothills of today's *Nepal* had a son. A seer warned him: this boy will become either a great king... or a great holy man.
N: The king wanted a king. So he gave his son, *Siddhartha*, a perfect palace. No sickness. No old age. No death. Nothing sad, ever.
N: It worked for twenty-nine years.
N: Then Siddhartha slipped out. And in one afternoon, he saw an old man, a sick man, a dead body... and a calm, wandering monk.
N: Soon after, he left the palace, his wife and his baby son, to find out why people suffer.
N: Years later, under a fig tree at *Bodh Gaya*, he sat down and refused to get up until he understood. When he rose, he was the *Buddha*: "the awakened one". His answer was a *middle way*. Not luxury. Not starvation. A calm, kind, disciplined life.
N: That's the story Buddhists have told for more than two thousand years.
N: And here's the strange part. Around the same time, another prince, *Mahavira*, walked away from his throne too, and taught *non-violence* to every living thing. Jainism grew from his teaching.
N: Two princes. Zero palaces. Very confused royal parents.
N: Remember our question about robbers? Here's the one treasure nobody could steal. Ideas. Buddhism would travel from this land to China, Japan and all of Southeast Asia, further than any army.
N: But armies were coming the other way. Far to the west, a young king from Macedonia had just conquered the mightiest empire in the world. And he wanted more.

# CHAPTER 3: THE MAN WHO REFUSED TO TIE HIS HAIR

N: His name was *Alexander*. In three twenty-six BCE, he crossed into the *Punjab*.
N: On the banks of the *Jhelum* river, a local king called *Porus* was waiting, with something the Greeks had rarely faced: *war elephants*. Keep those elephants in mind.
N: Alexander won. And the Greek writers tell a famous story about what came next.
[SKIT: riverbank after battle, Alexander in Greek armour seated, a wounded but upright Porus, a Greek scribe with a wax tablet]
ALEXANDER: How do you wish to be treated?
PORUS: Like a king.
SCRIBE: [whispering] Sir, he lost.
ALEXANDER: [pause] ...Fair. Keep your kingdom.
N: Then Alexander pushed east, to the river *Beas*. And there, his army simply... *stopped*.
N: They had marched for eight years. The monsoon rain wouldn't stop. And they had heard what was waiting beyond the river: the *Nanda* kings of Magadha, with a gigantic army, thousands of elephants, and an even bigger treasury.
N: The soldiers refused to go any further. Alexander, who had never lost a battle, had to turn back. He died in Babylon three years later, at thirty-two.
N: But a young man in India had been watching the Greeks very carefully. And he had a teacher with a grudge.
N: The teacher was a sharp, ruthless Brahmin named *Chanakya*. He had been insulted at the Nanda court and thrown out in front of everyone. So he untied the knot of his hair, and swore he would not tie it again until the Nandas were destroyed.
N: That's the oath the old stories give him. And he found exactly the weapon he needed: a young man named *Chandragupta Maurya*.
[SKIT: forest camp at night, Chanakya with wild untied hair, young Chandragupta practising with a sword, a nervous servant holding a comb]
SERVANT: Master, shall I bring a comb?
CHANAKYA: Not until the Nandas fall.
SERVANT: [looking at the hair] And... how long might that be?
CHANAKYA: [nodding at Chandragupta] That depends on him.
N: Around three twenty-one BCE, they did it. Chandragupta and Chanakya overthrew the Nandas and took their capital, *Pataliputra*, today's Patna.
N: Then Chandragupta did what nobody had done before. He joined most of the subcontinent into one state: the *Maurya Empire*.
N: When one of Alexander's generals marched in to take back the Indian lands, Chandragupta pushed him out, and then made a very Indian deal. The Greek general got *five hundred war elephants*. Chandragupta got the land.
N: A Greek ambassador who lived at Pataliputra described a giant city with wooden walls, hundreds of towers and a moat. And Chanakya is credited with the *Arthashastra*, a handbook for running a state: taxes, trade, war... and a lot of spies.
N: Remember our question? The first great conqueror to reach India from the west didn't stay. His army went home. The one who stayed was the young man who'd been watching him.
N: Chandragupta's empire was huge. But his grandson would make it even bigger... and then do something almost no conqueror in history has ever done. He would say sorry.

# CHAPTER 4: THE EMPEROR WHO APOLOGISED IN STONE

N: His name was *Ashoka*. And early on, he was not a gentle man. He fought his way to the throne, and he wanted more land.
N: In about two sixty-one BCE, he invaded *Kalinga*, on the east coast, in today's Odisha. He won. And then he counted the cost.
N: We know this because Ashoka told us himself. In an inscription carved into rock, he says a *hundred thousand* people were killed, and a *hundred and fifty thousand* were carried away.
N: And then he wrote something almost no conqueror ever wrote. He was *sorry*. An emperor, apologising, in stone.
N: Ashoka turned to the teachings of the Buddha. He called his new policy *dhamma*: tolerance between religions, care for animals, wells and shade trees along the roads. He sent monks across Asia, and Buddhism spread to Sri Lanka and beyond.
N: He carved his messages on rocks and tall polished pillars across the empire. And then, for about two thousand years, nobody could read them.
N: Until eighteen thirty-seven, when a British scholar named *James Prinsep* cracked the script, and Ashoka suddenly spoke again.
[SKIT: 1830s Calcutta study at night, James Prinsep surrounded by inscription rubbings, a sleepy clerk holding a lamp]
CLERK: Sir, it's three in the morning.
PRINSEP: [excited] This phrase keeps repeating. "Beloved of the gods, Piyadasi."
CLERK: Who's Piyadasi?
PRINSEP: [triumphant] A king who's been trying to talk to us for two thousand years.
N: One of Ashoka's pillars, at *Sarnath*, was topped with *four lions*, back to back. You've seen them. They're on every Indian banknote and passport.
N: And under the lions sits a wheel: the *Ashoka Chakra*. Keep that wheel in mind. Two thousand years later, it's going to win a contest.
N: But empires built by one great man rarely outlive him for long. About fifty years after Ashoka died, the last Maurya emperor was killed by his own general, in the middle of a military parade. The empire broke apart.
N: And yet, India didn't get poorer. The next few centuries would make it *richer than ever*. Because gold had started flowing in from a place that simply couldn't stop buying: *Rome*.

# CHAPTER 5: THE GOLDEN BIRD

N: Around two thousand years ago, ships sailed from Egypt to India's southern coast, riding the *monsoon winds*. They went home loaded with *pepper*, gems and *cotton cloth*.
N: And Rome paid in gold. So much gold that the Roman writer *Pliny* complained about it. He grumbled that India drained the empire of *fifty million sesterces* every single year. Hoards of Roman coins still turn up in south India today.
N: Keep Pliny's complaint in mind. Seventeen centuries later, someone in India is going to make exactly the same complaint... the other way round.
N: In the north, newcomers from Central Asia, the *Kushans*, built an empire, and their greatest king became a champion of Buddhism. Remember our question? They came as outsiders. They stayed, and became part of India.
N: Then, around three twenty CE, a new family united the north: the *Guptas*. And what people remember about the Gupta age isn't its battles. It's its ideas.
N: In four ninety-nine, a twenty-three-year-old mathematician named *Aryabhata* wrote that the Earth *spins* on its own axis. That's about a thousand years before Copernicus.
[SKIT: rooftop at night, young Aryabhata pointing at the stars, an older court astronomer frowning, a servant holding a lamp]
ASTRONOMER: So the sky turns around the Earth every night?
ARYABHATA: No. The Earth turns. The sky stays still.
ASTRONOMER: Then why am I not dizzy?
ARYABHATA: [patiently] I'll explain tomorrow. If the Earth is still here.
N: Aryabhata also worked out pi to four decimal places: three point one four one six.
N: And Indian mathematicians were building the number system you use every single day: nine digits, plus a sign for *nothing*. A little later, another mathematician wrote down the rules for *zero* as a number in its own right.
N: Those numerals travelled to the Arab world, and then to Europe. Which is why Europeans called them... *Arabic numerals*. The numbers on your phone are Indian.
N: This age gave the world something else too: a war game on an eight-by-eight board, with elephants, chariots, horses and foot soldiers. *Chaturanga*. Carried west through Persia, it became the game we call *chess*.
N: Students crossed Asia to study at *Nalanda*, one of the world's first great universities. And in Delhi stands an iron pillar from this age that has stood in the rain for about *sixteen hundred years*... and has barely rusted.
N: In the far south, the *Chola* kings later built one of the greatest navies in Asian history, and sent it across the sea to attack kingdoms in Sumatra and Malaya.
N: This was the land Indians still call, in its richest times, *Sone ki Chidiya*: the *Golden Bird*.
N: And a bird that shiny gets noticed. In Afghanistan, a powerful sultan had just learned exactly how much gold was sitting inside India's temples.

# CHAPTER 6: THE MAN WHO LOST AND CAME BACK

N: His name was *Mahmud of Ghazni*. Starting around the year one thousand, he raided north India again and again. About *seventeen* times.
N: He came for gold. His raiders stripped cities and temples, including the famous temple of *Somnath* in Gujarat, in ten twenty-six. Then he went home, his camels loaded. Keep that picture in your mind: India's gold, leaving on someone else's camels.
N: Mahmud never stayed. But about a hundred and fifty years later, another raider from Afghanistan arrived with a much bigger idea. He didn't just want India's gold. He wanted *India*.
N: His name was *Muhammad of Ghor*. And in eleven ninety-one, at *Tarain*, near Delhi, he ran into the Rajput king *Prithviraj Chauhan*.
N: Prithviraj crushed him. Ghori was badly wounded, and his own men carried him off the field.
[SKIT: Rajput camp after victory, a relaxed Prithviraj Chauhan, an eager young general holding a spear]
GENERAL: Shall we chase them, Your Majesty?
PRITHVIRAJ: [waving it off] Let them go. A man who loses like that doesn't come back.
GENERAL: [unsure] And if he does?
PRITHVIRAJ: Then we'll beat him again.
N: Whatever he really said, he didn't finish the job.
N: A year later, Ghori came back. Same battlefield. A bigger, better-trained army. This time, *Prithviraj lost*. Rule one of history: if you beat someone, make sure they don't come back next year.
N: In twelve oh six, one of Ghori's generals became the first sultan of *Delhi*, and began the tallest brick minaret in the world: the *Qutb Minar*. You can still see it in Delhi today.
N: In these same years, *Nalanda* was sacked, and its great libraries were lost.
N: And here's where our question really begins. Mahmud came for the gold and went home. These newcomers came for the gold... and *stayed*.
N: For the next three hundred years, five different families would rule from Delhi. And they were about to discover that ruling India was very different from robbing it. Especially the strangest sultan of them all.

# CHAPTER 7: BRILLIANT IDEAS, TERRIBLE TIMING

N: The Delhi sultans faced the enemy the rest of Asia feared most: the *Mongols*. China, Persia and Russia all fell to them.
N: Delhi didn't. One sultan threw back invasion after invasion. He also fixed the prices of grain, horses and cloth in the markets of Delhi, and punished cheating shopkeepers harshly. One of history's first price regulators. A very, very strict one.
N: But the sultan everyone remembers came a little later. *Muhammad bin Tughlaq*. Clever. Well-read. Full of big ideas. And with possibly the worst timing of any ruler in Indian history.
N: In thirteen twenty-seven, he decided his capital was in the wrong place. So he moved it from Delhi to *Daulatabad*, about a thousand kilometres south. And he didn't just move the court. He ordered much of Delhi's population to move with him.
[SKIT: Delhi street, Tughlaq on a palace balcony, a crowd of citizens holding bundles, a court official with a scroll]
TUGHLAQ: Great news! We are moving the capital. Everybody pack.
OFFICIAL: Everybody, Your Majesty?
TUGHLAQ: Everybody.
CITIZEN: [raising a hand] It's... forty days' walk.
TUGHLAQ: Then start walking.
N: A few years later, he moved everyone *back*.
N: Then he had another idea. He issued *copper and brass coins*, and declared them worth the same as silver.
N: Within months, people across the land were making coins at home. Blacksmiths, farmers, anyone with a furnace. Piles of fake money. The sultan had to buy them all back... with *real silver*.
N: Money backed only by the state's promise. A great idea. About five hundred years too early.
N: Then, in thirteen ninety-eight, the Central Asian conqueror *Timur* swept in and sacked Delhi. He took its treasure, its craftsmen, even its elephants, back to Samarkand. The city took a century to recover. Remember Timur. His family isn't finished with India.
N: But here's what was happening while sultans came and went. The newcomers were *changing*.
N: Persian and Indian builders mixed their styles in arches, domes and minarets. The poet *Amir Khusrau* wrote in a blend of Persian and the local speech, and is credited with shaping *qawwali* music.
N: And out of Delhi's bazaars and army camps, a whole new language grew: what would become *Urdu*, close cousin of Hindi.
N: Remember our question? They came as raiders. Within a few generations, they were eating India's food, singing India's songs, and speaking a language that could only have been born here.
N: Far to the south, a Hindu empire, *Vijayanagara*, was building a capital that foreign visitors compared to Rome.
N: Then, in fifteen twenty-six, a young prince rode down from Kabul. He had lost his kingdom more than once, and he was looking for a new one. On his father's side, he was the great-great-great-grandson of *Timur*.

# CHAPTER 8: THE EMPEROR WHO COULDN'T READ

N: His name was *Babur*. At *Panipat*, north of Delhi, he faced the sultan of Delhi, who had a far bigger army... and around a hundred *war elephants*.
N: Remember those elephants? For two thousand years, they had decided India's battles. Porus had them. The Nandas had thousands. Chandragupta traded land for five hundred.
N: Babur brought something new: *cannons and muskets*. The guns roared, the elephants panicked, and in a single day, the Sultanate was gone. So was the age of the war elephant.
N: Babur founded the *Mughal Empire*. He also wrote a brutally honest memoir. And honestly? He was not impressed with India.
[SKIT: Mughal camp, Babur lounging on cushions, a servant presenting a golden platter of mangoes]
SERVANT: Your Majesty, the finest mangoes in Hindustan!
BABUR: [unimpressed] No melons?
SERVANT: Mangoes, Majesty. Everyone loves them.
BABUR: [sighing] No good horses. No good grapes. No melons.
N: That complaint really is in his memoir.
N: If you're enjoying this, hit subscribe. We can't promise melons.
N: Babur ruled for only four years. His son *Humayun* lost the empire to an Afghan general, *Sher Shah Suri*, who in just five years rebuilt the great road across the north and issued a new silver coin. He called it the *rupiya*. The *rupee*.
N: Humayun won Delhi back... and a few months later, slipped on the stairs of his library, and died.
N: His son was thirteen years old. His name was *Akbar*. And Akbar never learned to read.
N: Yet he built a library of thousands of books, and had them read aloud to him, every single day.
N: In fifteen fifty-six, at a second battle at Panipat, his army won him the throne. Then Akbar did something smarter than winning battles. He *included* people.
N: He made alliances with the Rajput kingdoms, many through marriage, and gave Rajput princes top posts in his army. He scrapped the *jizya*, a tax on non-Muslims. His finance minister measured the land and fixed fairer taxes.
N: At his new city, *Fatehpur Sikri*, he built a hall where Muslim, Hindu, Jain, Zoroastrian and Christian scholars argued about religion in front of him.
[SKIT: debate hall, Akbar seated high, a Jesuit priest and a Brahmin pandit arguing, a court servant holding a bell]
SERVANT: [ringing the bell] One at a time, please!
PANDIT: He started it!
PRIEST: I did not!
AKBAR: [rubbing his temples] I invited you here to find the truth. Not a winner.
N: Not everyone bowed. *Maharana Pratap* of Mewar fought Akbar's army at *Haldighati*, and kept resisting from the hills for the rest of his life.
N: But by the time Akbar died, in sixteen oh five, the Mughal Empire was enormous. Remember our question? Babur rode in from Central Asia, missing his melons. His grandson was as Indian as the land he ruled.
N: And Akbar's own grandson would sit on the most expensive throne the world had ever seen.

# CHAPTER 9: THE MAN WHO BUILT THE TAJ

N: His name was *Shah Jahan*. And under him, the Mughal Empire reached the peak of its wealth and its show.
N: He sat on the *Peacock Throne*, made of gold and covered in jewels. And set into it was a diamond the size of a small egg: the *Koh-i-Noor*, the "Mountain of Light". Remember the diamond from the beginning? This is where its journey starts.
N: In this age, India was one of the richest places on Earth. And the secret wasn't diamonds. It was *cloth*. Remember the cotton from the Indus? Indian cotton now dressed people from Japan to Africa to London. Silver poured in from Europe and the Americas to pay for it.
N: When Shah Jahan's favourite wife, *Mumtaz Mahal*, died giving birth to their fourteenth child, he built her a tomb on the river at Agra.
N: Around twenty thousand workers and craftsmen. More than twenty years. White marble from Rajasthan, inlaid with stones from across Asia. The *Taj Mahal*.
N: Now, let's bust a myth. You've probably heard that once it was finished, Shah Jahan cut off the builders' hands, so they could never build anything as beautiful again. There's *no evidence* for it. No record from the time mentions it. It's a much later legend.
N: But Shah Jahan's ending was dark enough without it. His four sons fought a brutal war for the throne. The winner was *Aurangzeb*.
N: Aurangzeb had his brothers killed, and locked his own father inside the fort at Agra. From its windows, Shah Jahan could see the Taj across the river.
[SKIT: Agra Fort balcony at dusk, old Shah Jahan by the window, an elderly servant carrying a tray]
SERVANT: Your dinner, Majesty.
SHAH JAHAN: [not turning] Is my son coming to visit?
SERVANT: [hesitating] He sends... his regards.
SHAH JAHAN: [quietly] Regards. I gave him an empire.
N: Aurangzeb ruled for nearly fifty years, and pushed the empire to its largest size ever. But he spent his last twenty-five years at war in the south, and he brought back the jizya tax.
N: His toughest enemy was a young *Maratha* leader from the hills near Pune: *Shivaji*, a master of fast raids and hill forts. Placed under house arrest in Agra, he escaped, as the famous story goes, hidden inside giant baskets of sweets.
N: After Aurangzeb died in seventeen oh seven, the empire cracked. Governors became kings. The Marathas spread across the centre of India.
N: And in seventeen thirty-nine, the Persian ruler *Nadir Shah* marched in and sacked Delhi. He carried home the *Peacock Throne*... and the *Koh-i-Noor*.
N: Remember Mahmud's camels? India's treasure was leaving again. But the next visitors weren't interested in thrones. They didn't come with armies. They came with *account books*.

# CHAPTER 10: THE COMPANY

N: Remember the trading company from the start? Here it is.
N: On the last day of sixteen hundred, *Queen Elizabeth the First* gave a charter to a group of London merchants: the *East India Company*.
N: At first, they just wanted to trade. And what they wanted most was that Indian cloth. Painted, printed cotton, so popular in England that its names entered the English language: *calico*, from Calicut, and *chintz*, from the Hindi *chheent*.
N: It sold so well that English weavers rioted, and Parliament passed laws to *ban* Indian printed cloth.
N: The Company set up trading posts at *Surat, Madras, Bombay* and *Calcutta*. Bombay, by the way, came to England as part of a royal *wedding dowry*, when a Portuguese princess married King Charles the Second. Imagine getting Mumbai as a wedding gift.
N: To protect its trade, the Company hired soldiers. Then more soldiers. Then a lot more.
[SKIT: London boardroom, bewigged Company directors around a table with a giant ledger, a junior clerk reading the accounts]
DIRECTOR: Profits look good this year.
CLERK: Excellent, sir. And this line here: "Cannons. Elephants. Regiments."
DIRECTOR: Oh, that's the army.
CLERK: [confused] We're a trading company. Why do we have an army?
DIRECTOR: [shrugging] Why don't the others?
N: By the early eighteen hundreds, the Company's private army in India was about *two hundred thousand* strong. Bigger than the British Army itself.
N: Its rise began with a bored young clerk in Madras who found out he was much better at war than at paperwork: *Robert Clive*.
N: In seventeen fifty-seven, at *Plassey* in Bengal, Clive's small army faced the *Nawab of Bengal*. But Clive had already made a secret deal with the Nawab's own commander. When the battle began, much of the Nawab's army simply... *stood still*.
N: One of the most important battles in Indian history was won by a *deal*.
N: Within a few years, the Company won the right to collect the taxes of Bengal, one of the richest provinces on Earth. A company now ruled millions of people. And its first priority was *profit*.
N: In seventeen seventy, a terrible famine struck Bengal. Some estimates say up to a *third* of the population died. The Company didn't cut its taxes. It *raised* them.
N: In these same years, English picked up a new word from India: *loot*. From the Hindi *lut*. Plunder.
N: Not everyone gave in. *Tipu Sultan* of Mysore fought the Company with iron-cased rockets so good that the British copied them. In the Punjab, *Ranjit Singh* built a Sikh empire that kept the Company out for forty years. He even got the *Koh-i-Noor*.
N: But after he died, the Company took the Punjab. And the diamond went to *Queen Victoria*.
N: Remember our question? Every robber before had stayed, and become part of India. This one kept its headquarters in London.
N: By the eighteen-fifties, the Company controlled most of India. And the soldiers it relied on most, the *sepoys*, were Indian. That was about to matter. A lot.

# CHAPTER 11: THE QUEEN THEY TRIED TO RETIRE

N: In eighteen fifty-seven, the Company gave its Indian soldiers a new rifle. To load it, you had to *bite open* a paper cartridge. A rumour spread like fire: the cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindus and Muslims alike.
N: At *Meerut*, soldiers who refused them were jailed. Their comrades rose up, freed them, and marched to Delhi, where they declared the old Mughal emperor, *Bahadur Shah Zafar*, ruler of India again.
N: The revolt spread across the north. And one of its fiercest leaders was a young widow the Company had tried to *retire*.
N: *Rani Lakshmibai* of *Jhansi*. When her husband died, the Company refused to accept her adopted son as heir, and simply took her kingdom. She was handed a pension and told to step aside.
N: In eighteen fifty-seven, she stepped back in. She led her troops on horseback, and died fighting, in her late twenties. She became the most famous face of the revolt.
N: The British crushed the rising with terrible brutality. Whole villages were burned. Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled to Burma. The Mughal dynasty, after three hundred and thirty years, was over.
N: London decided the Company could not be trusted with India anymore. In eighteen fifty-eight, the *British Crown* took over directly. The *Raj* had begun.
[SKIT: Windsor Castle drawing room, Queen Victoria at a writing desk, a nervous minister holding a document]
MINISTER: Your Majesty, India is now ruled by the Crown.
VICTORIA: Splendid. And the Company?
MINISTER: Closed, Your Majesty.
VICTORIA: Then I shall need a new title.
MINISTER: "Empress of India", Ma'am?
VICTORIA: [smiling] Now *that's* a title.
N: She got it, in eighteen seventy-six.
N: India gave Britain smaller things too. In eighteen fourteen, an Indian businessman opened a bath house in Brighton offering a head massage called *champo*. Today you'd call it *shampoo*.
N: Under the Raj, India got *railways*. Remember those bricks from Harappa? Same railway age. The tracks ran mainly to carry raw cotton to the ports, and British factory cloth back in.
N: Think about that. India, the land of calico and chintz, the land that once dressed half the world, now bought its cloth from *Manchester*.
N: And the famines kept coming. In the Great Famine of eighteen seventy-six, millions died while grain was still being exported.
N: Then an Indian thinker named *Dadabhai Naoroji* sat down with the numbers. Remember Pliny, the Roman who complained that India drained Rome of its gold? Naoroji made the same complaint, the other way round. He called it the *drain of wealth*: India's money flowing out to Britain, year after year.
N: But the British also built schools, newspapers and universities. And in them, a new generation of Indians learned exactly how to argue with an empire. One of them was a young lawyer, about to be thrown off a train.

# CHAPTER 12: A LAWYER THROWN OFF A TRAIN

N: June, eighteen ninety-three. A young Indian lawyer is sitting in a *first-class* compartment on a train in *South Africa*. He has a valid ticket. A white passenger complains. At *Pietermaritzburg* station, he is thrown off the train.
N: He spent that night shivering in the station waiting room, deciding whether to go home or to fight. He stayed, and fought, for more than twenty years.
N: His name was *Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi*. When he came home to India in nineteen fifteen, people would come to call him *Mahatma*, "great soul".
N: His weapon was *satyagraha*: holding on to the truth, and resisting without violence.
N: In nineteen nineteen, in *Amritsar*, thousands of people gathered peacefully in a walled garden called *Jallianwala Bagh*. A British general blocked the exit and ordered his troops to fire into the crowd. They fired until they ran out of bullets. The official count was three hundred and seventy-nine dead. Indian estimates were over a thousand.
N: After Amritsar, millions of Indians who had trusted the British stopped trusting them.
N: And Gandhi chose the most unlikely weapon in history. Remember that Indian cloth? Remember Manchester? Gandhi told Indians to *burn* foreign cloth, and spin their own.
N: He sat at a *spinning wheel*, the *charkha*, every day, and wore rough homespun cotton called *khadi*. The spinning wheel went onto the freedom movement's flag. The cloth that had once made India rich would now help set it free.
N: In nineteen thirty, he picked an even stranger target: *salt*. The British taxed it, and Indians were banned from making their own. So Gandhi walked to the sea.
[SKIT: Dandi beach, Gandhi in khadi bending to pick up salt crystals, a flustered British police officer with a notebook, marchers behind]
OFFICER: Mr Gandhi, what exactly are you doing?
GANDHI: [calmly] Picking up salt.
OFFICER: That's... illegal.
GANDHI: Then arrest me. And the thousands behind me.
OFFICER: [looking at the crowd, then his notebook] I'll need a bigger notebook.
N: The Salt March covered about *three hundred and eighty kilometres* in twenty-four days, and newspapers around the world followed every step.
N: Not everyone believed in non-violence. *Bhagat Singh* took up arms, and was hanged at twenty-three. *Subhas Chandra Bose* raised an army with Japan's help. And *Dr B R Ambedkar*, born into a so-called untouchable caste, fought for India's most oppressed people.
N: In the Second World War, about *two and a half million* Indian soldiers fought for Britain, the largest volunteer army in history. Meanwhile, in nineteen forty-three, a famine in Bengal killed around *three million* people.
N: By the end of the war, Britain was broke and exhausted. It could no longer hold on to India.
N: Freedom was coming. But it was not coming *whole*.

# CHAPTER 13: FIVE WEEKS TO DIVIDE A COUNTRY

N: The British lawyer *Cyril Radcliffe* had never been to India. In the summer of nineteen forty-seven, he was given about *five weeks* to draw the line that would split it.
N: The Congress and the *Muslim League*, led by *Muhammad Ali Jinnah*, could not agree on one country. The League demanded a separate homeland for Muslims: *Pakistan*. Britain agreed to divide the land.
[SKIT: hot Delhi office 1947, Radcliffe at a desk under a slow ceiling fan, piles of maps, an assistant holding census papers]
ASSISTANT: Sir, the census figures for the Punjab.
RADCLIFFE: [wiping his glasses] And how long do I have?
ASSISTANT: About five weeks.
RADCLIFFE: To divide... how many people?
ASSISTANT: Nearly four hundred million, sir.
N: At midnight on the *fifteenth of August, nineteen forty-seven*, India became free. In Delhi, *Jawaharlal Nehru*, the first Prime Minister, told the assembly that at the stroke of midnight, while the world slept, India would awake to life and freedom.
N: But Radcliffe's line was only announced *two days later*. Across Punjab and Bengal, millions of people woke up on the "wrong" side of a border they had never seen.
N: What followed was one of the largest migrations in human history. Somewhere between *ten and twenty million* people were forced from their homes. Trains arrived carrying only the dead. Estimates of those killed range from two hundred thousand to *two million*.
N: Gandhi spent Independence Day not in Delhi, but in *Calcutta*, fasting to stop the killing. Five months later, on the thirtieth of January, nineteen forty-eight, he was shot dead by a Hindu extremist.
N: India had another enormous problem. More than *five hundred and sixty* princely states, each ruled by its own maharaja or nawab, had to choose where to go. The man in charge was the Home Minister, *Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel*.
[SKIT: palace hall, a bejewelled maharaja on a throne, Patel in a simple white dhoti seated opposite, a document on the table between them]
MAHARAJA: My family has ruled here for three hundred years.
PATEL: And India would be honoured to keep you comfortable for three hundred more.
MAHARAJA: And in return?
PATEL: [sliding the document across] One signature.
N: Most signed. Hyderabad held out until the Indian army moved in. Kashmir's ruler signed after raiders from Pakistan invaded, and the first war between India and Pakistan began.
N: Then India wrote its rules. A committee led by *Dr Ambedkar* drafted the *Constitution*, the longest written constitution of any country. It came into force on the twenty-sixth of January, nineteen fifty: *Republic Day*.
N: And remember Ashoka's wheel? When India chose its national flag, there were two wheels in the running. Gandhi's *spinning wheel* had been on the freedom flag for years. But in July nineteen forty-seven, the Constituent Assembly replaced it with the *Ashoka Chakra*. The emperor who apologised in stone, back at the centre of the flag, after two thousand years.
N: The new republic also did something many rich democracies took centuries to do: it gave *every adult* the vote, from day one. In the first election, about a hundred and seventy million people were eligible, and most of them could not read. So every party got a *picture symbol*.
N: The biggest election the world had ever seen, run on pictures.
N: India was free. It was also *poor*. And it was about to find out how close to broke a country can get.

# CHAPTER 14: FORTY-SEVEN TONNES OF GOLD

N: The young republic had a rough start. It lost a border war with *China* in nineteen sixty-two, and fought Pakistan in nineteen sixty-five. By the mid-sixties, India was so short of food that it depended on grain shipments from America.
N: Then came the *Green Revolution*: new high-yield wheat and rice, irrigation and fertiliser. Within about a decade, India could feed itself.
N: In nineteen seventy-one, India backed the independence struggle in East Pakistan and won a short war. A new country was born: *Bangladesh*.
N: In nineteen seventy-five, Prime Minister *Indira Gandhi* declared an *Emergency*. Elections were suspended, opponents jailed, newspapers censored. And when she finally called elections, the voters, the same voters of the picture symbols, *threw her out*.
N: For most of these years, India's economy was tightly controlled. You needed government permission to make almost anything. People called it the *Licence Raj*.
N: In nineteen ninety-one, it all came to a head. India nearly ran out of money. Its foreign reserves could pay for only about *three weeks* of imports.
N: To get an emergency loan, the government did something unthinkable. It flew *forty-seven tonnes* of its own gold to the *Bank of England*.
N: Remember Mahmud's camels, carrying India's gold away? Remember Naoroji's drain? Now India had to *send* its own gold to London.
[SKIT: night airport tarmac 1991, Indian officials loading heavy crates onto a cargo plane, a young clerk with a clipboard]
CLERK: Cargo: forty-seven tonnes of gold. Destination: London.
OFFICIAL: Keep it quiet.
CLERK: Wait... didn't the British take enough already?
OFFICIAL: This time, we're getting it back.
N: And they did. Because that crisis forced a revolution. A quiet economist named *Manmohan Singh*, the new finance minister, stood up in Parliament and quoted Victor Hugo: no power on Earth can stop an idea whose time has come.
N: India opened up. Lower taxes on imports. Private companies welcome. Foreign investment allowed. Growth took off, and software companies in *Bengaluru* and *Hyderabad* began serving the whole world.
N: In two thousand fourteen, India reached *Mars* on its very first try, for about seventy-four million dollars. Less than the Hollywood space film *Gravity* cost to make.
N: In two thousand twenty-three, *Chandrayaan-3* became the first spacecraft to land near the Moon's south pole. That same year, India became the most populous country on Earth.
N: *Narendra Modi* has been Prime Minister since two thousand fourteen. Today India is the world's *fifth-largest economy*, and the IMF expects it to pass Japan next year.
N: To be fair, it still has a long way to go. Hundreds of millions still earn very little. Good jobs are scarce for the young. And critics worry about pressure on the press and on religious minorities.
N: But the Golden Bird is climbing again.

# ENDING: THE ANSWER

N: So think about everyone who came to take something from this land. Alexander. The Kushans. Mahmud. Ghori. Timur. Babur. Nadir Shah. A company from London.
N: Think about everything they carried off: gold on camels, a peacock throne, a diamond that now sits in the *Tower of London*, and a drain of wealth that ran for two hundred years.
N: And now, the question from the start. Why did almost every robber end up *staying*?
N: Here's the answer. India's *wealth* could always be carried away. India *itself* never could.
N: The Kushans became champions of Indian Buddhism. The sultans' soldiers gave India Urdu and qawwali. Babur arrived missing his melons, and his great-great-grandson built the Taj Mahal. Again and again, the invaders ended up eating India's food, speaking its languages, and building its monuments.
N: Only one never moved in. It ruled from an office in London, and it sent the money *home*. By the time it left, India's share of the world had fallen to about four percent.‡
N: Remember that sailor at Calicut, more than five hundred years ago? He came for spices, and for cloth so fine that Europe named it after the port where he stood.
N: Today, the world is coming back to buy from India again. Its software, its medicines, and yes, its cloth. India is once again one of the biggest cloth-makers on Earth.
N: The Golden Bird lost its gold more times than anyone can count. It never lost itself.

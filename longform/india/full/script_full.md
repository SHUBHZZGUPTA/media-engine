# CHAPTER 2: THE HYMNS

N: Clive's army was much smaller. But he had secretly made a deal with the Nawab's own commander, *Mir Jafar*. When the battle began, much of the Nawab's army simply... stood still.
TRAVELLER: [facepalm] One of the most important battles in Indian history. Won by a betrayal.
N: Within a few years, the Company won the right to collect the taxes of Bengal, one of the richest provinces in the world.
N: A company now ruled millions of people. And its first priority was profit.
N: In *seventeen seventy*, a terrible famine struck Bengal. Some estimates say up to a *third* of the population died. Tax collection barely slowed.
N: Not everyone gave in. In the south, the kingdom of *Mysore*, under *Hyder Ali* and his son *Tipu Sultan*, fought the Company in four wars. Its army used iron-cased *rockets* so effective that the British studied them and built their own.
N: Tipu died defending his capital in *seventeen ninety-nine*.
N: In the Punjab, *Maharaja Ranjit Singh* built a strong Sikh empire and kept the Company out for forty years. He even owned the Koh-i-Noor.
N: But after he died, the Company fought two wars with the Sikhs. In *eighteen forty-nine*, it took the Punjab... and the diamond went to Queen Victoria. It is still in London today, and India has asked for it back.
TRAVELLER: [sighing] The diamond's been on quite a journey.
N: By the eighteen-fifties, the Company controlled most of India, directly or through puppet kings.
N: And the soldiers it relied on most, the *sepoys*, were Indian. That was about to matter. A lot.

# CHAPTER 10: THE REVOLT AND THE RAJ

N: In *eighteen fifty-seven*, the Company gave its Indian soldiers a new rifle. To load it, you had to bite open a paper cartridge.
N: A rumour spread like fire: the cartridges were greased with *cow and pig fat*. Offensive to both Hindus and Muslims.
N: At *Meerut*, in May, soldiers who refused the cartridges were jailed. Their comrades rose up, freed them, and marched to Delhi.
N: There, they declared the old, powerless Mughal emperor, *Bahadur Shah Zafar*, the ruler of India again.
TRAVELLER: [nervously winding his watch] The Company has a problem.
N: The revolt spread across north India. In *Jhansi*, a young queen, *Rani Lakshmibai*, led her troops into battle on horseback, and died fighting.
N: The British crushed the rising with terrible brutality. Whole villages were burned. Rebels were tied to cannons and blown apart.
N: Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled to Rangoon in Burma. The *Mughal dynasty*, after three hundred and thirty years, was over.
N: And the British government decided the Company could not be trusted with India anymore. In *eighteen fifty-eight*, the British Crown took over directly. The *British Raj* had begun.

[SKIT: a London palace, Queen Victoria at a desk, a nervous minister holding a crown on a cushion]
MINISTER: Your Majesty, India is now ruled by the Crown.
VICTORIA: [pleased] Splendid. And the Company?
MINISTER: Closed, Your Majesty.
VICTORIA: [eyeing the crown] Then I shall need a new title.
MINISTER: [sweating] "Empress of India", Ma'am?
VICTORIA: [smiling] Now that's a title.

N: She did get it, eventually. In *eighteen seventy-six*, Queen Victoria was proclaimed *Empress of India*.

N: Under the Raj, India got railways, telegraph lines, universities and a new class of English-educated Indians.
N: The first passenger train ran in *eighteen fifty-three*, from Bombay to Thane. Remember the railway that buried the bricks of Harappa? Same era.
TRAVELLER: [small smile] Full circle. Kind of.
N: But the railways were built mainly to carry raw cotton to the ports, and British factory cloth back in.
N: India, once the world's great cloth-maker, now bought its cloth from Manchester.
N: And famines kept coming. In the Great Famine of *eighteen seventy-six*, millions died, while grain was still being exported.
N: An Indian thinker named *Dadabhai Naoroji* added up the numbers and gave it a name: the *drain of wealth*. Money flowing out of India, year after year.
N: Remember that quarter of the world's economy? By *nineteen fifty*, India's share had fallen to about *four percent*.
TRAVELLER: [quietly] The shop wasn't just unlocked. It was emptied.
N: But the same schools and newspapers the British built gave Indians the tools to fight back. Not with swords this time. With *ideas*.

# CHAPTER 11: THE FREEDOM STRUGGLE

N: In *eighteen eighty-five*, a group of educated Indians founded the *Indian National Congress*. At first, they just asked politely for more say.
N: In *nineteen oh five*, the British split Bengal in two. Bengal exploded in protest. People burned British cloth and bought Indian goods only: *Swadeshi*.
N: In *nineteen fifteen*, a lawyer came home from South Africa, where he had spent twenty years fighting discrimination against Indians.
N: He wore simple clothes and spoke softly. His name was *Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi*. People came to call him *Mahatma*, "great soul".
N: His weapon was *satyagraha*: holding on to the truth, and resisting without violence.
TRAVELLER: [puzzled] Fighting an empire without fighting. Let's see how this goes.
N: In *nineteen nineteen*, in *Amritsar*, thousands of people gathered peacefully in a walled garden called *Jallianwala Bagh*.
N: A British general, *Reginald Dyer*, blocked the only exit and ordered his troops to fire into the crowd. They fired until they ran out of bullets.
N: The official count said three hundred and seventy-nine dead. Indian estimates were over a *thousand*.
N: After Amritsar, millions of Indians who had trusted the British stopped trusting them.
N: Gandhi called on India to stop cooperating: leave British schools, courts and jobs, and burn foreign cloth.
N: In *nineteen thirty*, he chose an unlikely target: *salt*. The British taxed it, and Indians were banned from making their own.

[SKIT: a beach at Dandi at dawn, Gandhi bending down, a British police officer watching with a notebook]
OFFICER: [stern] Mr Gandhi, what exactly are you doing?
GANDHI: [holding up a pinch of salt] Picking up salt.
OFFICER: That's... illegal.
GANDHI: [calmly] Then arrest me. And the thousands behind me.
OFFICER: [looking at the crowd, gulping] I'll need a bigger notebook.

N: The *Salt March* covered about three hundred and eighty kilometres in twenty-four days. Newspapers around the world followed every step.
N: Not everyone believed in non-violence. Young revolutionaries like *Bhagat Singh* took up arms. He was hanged in *nineteen thirty-one*, at just twenty-three, and became a hero across India.
N: And *Dr B R Ambedkar*, born into a so-called "untouchable" caste, fought for the rights of India's most oppressed communities.
N: When the Second World War came, about *two and a half million* Indian soldiers fought for Britain. The largest volunteer army in history.
N: Meanwhile, *Subhas Chandra Bose* tried another path: he raised the *Indian National Army* with Japan's help, to fight the British from the east.
N: In *nineteen forty-two*, Gandhi gave Britain a simple message: *Quit India*. Almost the entire Congress leadership was thrown in jail.
N: In *nineteen forty-three*, a famine in Bengal killed around *three million* people.
TRAVELLER: [grim] The Golden Bird, starving.
N: By the end of the war, Britain was broke and exhausted. And it could no longer hold on to India.
N: Freedom was coming. But it was not coming whole.

# CHAPTER 12: MIDNIGHT

N: As freedom came closer, the deep split between the Congress and the *Muslim League*, led by *Muhammad Ali Jinnah*, could not be bridged. The League demanded a separate country for Muslims: *Pakistan*.
N: In the end, Britain agreed to divide the land.
N: The border was drawn by a British lawyer, *Cyril Radcliffe*, who had never been to India. He was given about five weeks.
TRAVELLER: [horrified] Five weeks. To divide four hundred million people.
N: At midnight on *the fifteenth of August, nineteen forty-seven*, India became free.
N: In Delhi, *Jawaharlal Nehru*, the first Prime Minister, told the assembly that India had made a *tryst with destiny*. At the stroke of midnight, while the world slept, India would awake to life and freedom.
N: But the border was only announced two days later. And across Punjab and Bengal, millions of people suddenly found themselves on the "wrong" side.
N: What followed was one of the largest migrations in human history. Somewhere between *ten and twenty million* people were forced to leave their homes.
N: And there was terrible violence on both sides. Estimates of the dead range from *two hundred thousand* to *two million*.
TRAVELLER: [quietly] Every one of those numbers was a family.
N: Gandhi spent Independence Day not in Delhi, but in Calcutta, fasting to stop the killing.
N: Five months later, on *the thirtieth of January, nineteen forty-eight*, he was shot dead in Delhi by a Hindu extremist.
N: And India still had another enormous problem. More than *five hundred and sixty* princely states, each ruled by its own maharaja or nawab, had to decide where to go.
N: The man in charge was the Home Minister, *Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel*, with his right-hand man, *V P Menon*.

[SKIT: a palace hall, a maharaja on a cushion, Patel standing with a single sheet of paper]
MAHARAJA: [grandly] My family has ruled here for three hundred years.
PATEL: [calmly] And India would be honoured to keep you comfortable for three hundred more.
MAHARAJA: [suspicious] And in return?
PATEL: [sliding the paper over] One signature.
MAHARAJA: [sighing, picking up the pen] You make it sound so easy.

N: Most rulers signed. Hyderabad held out until the Indian army moved in, in *nineteen forty-eight*. Kashmir's ruler signed after raiders from Pakistan invaded, and the first war between India and Pakistan began.
N: Patel became known as the *Iron Man of India*. *Stitch*. Bigger than any empire before.
N: Then India wrote its rules. A committee led by *Dr Ambedkar* drafted the *Constitution*: the longest written constitution of any country.
N: It came into force on *the twenty-sixth of January, nineteen fifty*. Republic Day.
N: And it did something many rich democracies took centuries to do. It gave every adult the vote, from day one. Rich or poor, man or woman, of every caste and religion.
N: In the first election, in *nineteen fifty-one*, about *a hundred and seventy million* people were eligible to vote. Most of them could not read. So each party had a picture symbol.
TRAVELLER: [grinning] The biggest election the world had ever seen. Run on pictures.
N: The Golden Bird was free. Now it had to learn to fly again.

# CHAPTER 13: THE COMEBACK

N: The young republic had a rough start. India lost a border war with *China* in *nineteen sixty-two*, and fought Pakistan in *nineteen sixty-five*.
N: In the mid-sixties, India was so short of food that it depended on grain shipments from America.
N: Then came the *Green Revolution*. New high-yield wheat and rice, irrigation and fertiliser. Within a decade, India was growing enough to feed itself.
TRAVELLER: [holding a bag of rice] From begging for grain to feeding a billion. Not bad.
N: In *nineteen seventy-one*, India backed the independence struggle in East Pakistan, and won a short war. A new country was born: *Bangladesh*.
N: In *nineteen seventy-five*, Prime Minister *Indira Gandhi* declared an *Emergency*. Elections were suspended, opposition leaders jailed, newspapers censored.
N: But when she finally called elections in *nineteen seventy-seven*, the voters threw her out.
TRAVELLER: [nodding] The voters remembered the picture symbols.
N: She came back to power in nineteen eighty. In *nineteen eighty-four*, she was assassinated, and terrible violence against Sikhs followed in Delhi and other cities.
N: For most of these years, India's economy was tightly controlled. You needed government permission to make almost anything. People called it the *Licence Raj*.
N: In *nineteen ninety-one*, India almost ran out of money. Its foreign reserves could pay for only about *three weeks* of imports.
N: To get an emergency loan, the government flew *forty-seven tonnes* of gold to the *Bank of England*.

[SKIT: a night-time airport runway, an official checking crates, a pilot holding a clipboard]
PILOT: [reading] Cargo: forty-seven tonnes of gold. Destination: London.
OFFICIAL: [whispering] Keep it quiet.
PILOT: [confused] Wait... didn't the British take enough already?
OFFICIAL: [sighing] This time, we're getting it back.

N: That crisis forced a revolution. Prime Minister *P V Narasimha Rao* and Finance Minister *Manmohan Singh* opened the economy: lower taxes on imports, private companies welcome, foreign investment allowed.
N: Then India's growth took off. Software companies in *Bengaluru* and *Hyderabad* began serving the whole world.
N: In *two thousand fourteen*, India reached *Mars* on its very first try. The mission cost about *seventy-four million dollars*. Less than the Hollywood space film *Gravity*.
TRAVELLER: [laughing] Cheaper than a movie about space. Actually in space.
N: In *two thousand twenty-three*, *Chandrayaan-3* became the first spacecraft to land near the Moon's *south pole*. That same year, India became the *most populous country on Earth*.
N: *Narendra Modi* has been Prime Minister since *two thousand fourteen*. His supporters point to new roads and airports, digital payments used by hundreds of millions, and fast growth.
N: His critics worry about jobs, inequality, and pressure on the press and on religious minorities.
N: And today, India is the world's fifth-largest economy in dollars. The IMF expects it to pass Japan in two thousand twenty-seven. Measured by what money can actually buy, it's already third.

# ENDING

N: So let's look back at the loop.
N: The Indus cities stitched together, shone, and went silent. The Mauryas stitched the land together, shone, and shattered. Then the Guptas. Then the Sultans. Then the Mughals.
N: Every time, outsiders marched in. And every time, India absorbed them. Their food, their words, their buildings, their gods all became part of the same story.
TRAVELLER: [winding his watch] Stitch. Shine. Shatter. Absorb.
N: Three hundred years ago, India made about a quarter of everything in the world. By nineteen fifty, about four percent.
N: Today, it's climbing back. Fast.
N: The question is no longer whether the Golden Bird can fly again. It's whether this time, India can *shine* without the *shatter*.
TRAVELLER: [looking up] I've seen how this story usually goes. But I've also seen this land surprise everyone. Many times.
N: What do you think? Can India break the loop?
TRAVELLER: [checking his watch, grinning] Next stop: China. They have a loop of their own.

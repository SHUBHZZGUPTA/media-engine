# India — script, chapters 2–7

Same format as the pilot: `N:` narrator · `TRAVELLER:` mascot · other names are skit characters. `*word*` = keyword caption. `[tag]` = delivery.
Legends and disputed details are framed as "the story goes" / "tradition says" / "historians debate". A Traveller or skit line at least every ~250 words (hidden take-switch points).

# CHAPTER 2: THE HYMNS

N: After the Indus cities faded, a new culture took shape in the north of India. We know it from its sacred hymns: the *Vedas*.
N: The oldest of them, the *Rigveda*, is more than *three thousand years old*. Over a thousand hymns to gods of fire, storm and sky.
N: Where did the people who composed them come from? This is one of the most argued-about questions in Indian history.
N: Many historians, using clues from languages and ancient DNA, think speakers of an *Indo-Aryan* language arrived gradually from Central Asia, mixing with people already living here.
N: Others argue Vedic culture grew mostly from within India itself. The debate is still going.
TRAVELLER: [hands up] I'm not touching this one. I'm just here for the hymns.
N: Here's what nobody argues about. For more than three thousand years, the Vedas were not written down. They were *memorised*.
N: Priests trained from childhood to chant them in special patterns: forwards, backwards, word pairs, word triplets. So that not a single sound would change.
N: It worked. Today the chanting is recognised by UNESCO as part of humanity's *intangible heritage*.

[SKIT: a forest hermitage at dawn, a teacher with a rudraksha mala, a young student cross-legged, yawning]
TEACHER: [calm] Again. From the beginning. Backwards this time.
STUDENT: [groaning] Guruji, we've done this hymn forty times.
TEACHER: And tomorrow we do it forty more.
STUDENT: [whispering] Can't we just write it down?
TEACHER: [eyebrow raised] Write it down? And trust a *leaf*?

N: Over centuries, these communities moved east into the great plain of the *Ganga*. They cleared forests, farmed rice, and learned to use *iron*.
N: Villages grew into towns. Towns into kingdoms. By about *six hundred BCE*, north India had sixteen big states, the *Mahajanapadas*.
N: Society was also hardening into ranked groups: priests, warriors, traders and workers. A system called *varna*, which, over many centuries, would set into the rigid rules of caste.
N: And the thinkers of this age asked bigger and bigger questions. In texts called the *Upanishads*, they asked: what is the self? What happens after death? Is there one reality behind everything?
TRAVELLER: [tapping his head] Big questions. No internet. Impressive.
N: Of all sixteen kingdoms, one was growing faster and hungrier than the rest: *Magadha*, in today's Bihar.
N: And two young princes of this age were about to walk away from their palaces... and change the world without fighting a single war.

# CHAPTER 3: THE PRINCE WHO LEFT

N: The first was a prince named *Siddhartha Gautama*, born around two and a half thousand years ago in *Lumbini*, in today's Nepal.
N: The story goes that his father, a king, wanted him to see nothing sad. No sickness. No old age. No death. Just a perfect palace.
N: But one day Siddhartha slipped out. And he saw an old man, a sick man, a dead body... and a calm wandering monk.
TRAVELLER: [quietly] One afternoon outside the palace. That's all it took.
N: At twenty-nine, he left his palace, his wife and his baby son, to find out why people suffer.
N: After years of searching, he sat under a fig tree at *Bodh Gaya* and, tradition says, refused to get up until he understood.
N: When he rose, he was the *Buddha*: "the awakened one". He gave his first sermon at *Sarnath*, near Varanasi.
N: His answer was a *middle way*. Not luxury. Not starving yourself. A calm, kind, disciplined life.
N: Around the same time, another prince, *Mahavira*, gave up his throne too. He taught *ahimsa*, non-violence to every living thing, and shaped the religion of *Jainism*.
TRAVELLER: [counting on fingers] Two princes. Zero palaces. Very confused royal parents.
N: Meanwhile, Magadha kept growing. Its new rulers, the *Nanda* kings, were famous for two things: a gigantic army... and an even bigger treasury.
N: And far to the west, a young king from Macedonia had just conquered the mightiest empire in the world, Persia. He wanted more.
N: His name was *Alexander*.
N: In *three twenty-six BCE*, Alexander crossed into the Punjab. On the banks of the *Jhelum* river he fought a king called *Porus*, whose army brought something the Greeks had rarely faced: *war elephants*.
N: Alexander won. But the Greek writers tell a famous story about what came next.

[SKIT: a battlefield tent by a river, Alexander in a plumed helmet, Porus standing tall, wounded but proud]
ALEXANDER: [curious] How do you wish to be treated?
PORUS: [calmly] Like a king.
ALEXANDER: [impressed pause] ...Fair. Keep your kingdom.

N: Then Alexander pushed east, to the river *Beas*. And there, his army simply... stopped.
N: They had marched for eight years. They had heard about the Nanda army waiting beyond: thousands of elephants, hundreds of thousands of soldiers. And the monsoon rain would not stop.
N: They refused to go any further. Alexander, who had never lost a battle, had to turn back.
TRAVELLER: [whistling] Undefeated. Except by his own tired soldiers. And the weather.
N: Alexander died in Babylon three years later, at thirty-two. He never saw India again.
N: But a young man in India had been watching the Greeks very carefully... and he had a teacher with a plan.

# CHAPTER 4: THE FIRST EMPIRE

N: The young man was *Chandragupta Maurya*. His teacher was a sharp, ruthless Brahmin called *Chanakya*, also known as *Kautilya*.
N: The story goes that Chanakya had been insulted at the Nanda court, and swore he would not tie up his hair until the Nandas were destroyed.
TRAVELLER: [nervous] Rule one of ancient India: never insult the guy with the untied hair.
N: Around *three twenty-one BCE*, Chandragupta and Chanakya overthrew the Nandas and took Magadha's capital, *Pataliputra*, today's Patna.
N: Then Chandragupta did what nobody had done before. He united most of the subcontinent into one state. The *Maurya Empire*. *Stitch*.
N: When one of Alexander's generals, *Seleucus*, came to take back the Indian lands, Chandragupta pushed him out and made a deal: land in the northwest for *five hundred war elephants*.
N: A Greek ambassador, *Megasthenes*, lived at Pataliputra and described a huge city of wooden walls, with hundreds of towers and a moat around it.
N: Chanakya is traditionally credited with the *Arthashastra*, a handbook on how to run a state: taxes, spies, trade, war. Scholars still debate who exactly wrote it, and when.

[SKIT: a royal office in Pataliputra, Chanakya with a scroll, a junior minister holding a long list]
MINISTER: [reading] Sir, we have spies watching the merchants...
CHANAKYA: Good.
MINISTER: ...spies watching the spies...
CHANAKYA: Excellent.
MINISTER: [nervous] And who is watching *me*?
CHANAKYA: [smiling] Exactly.

N: Tradition says that in old age, Chandragupta gave up his throne, became a Jain monk, and died at *Shravanabelagola* in Karnataka.
N: His grandson became the most famous ruler in Indian history: *Ashoka*.
N: In about *two sixty-one BCE*, Ashoka invaded *Kalinga*, in today's Odisha. He won. And the cost horrified him.
N: We know this because Ashoka told us himself. In an inscription carved in stone, he says *a hundred thousand* people were killed, and a hundred and fifty thousand were carried away.
N: Then he wrote something almost no conqueror ever wrote: he was *sorry*.
TRAVELLER: [taking off his goggles] An emperor. Apologising. In stone. Read that twice.
N: Ashoka turned to the teachings of the Buddha. He called his new policy *dhamma*: kindness, tolerance between religions, care for animals, wells and shade trees along the roads.
N: He carved his messages on rocks and tall stone pillars across the empire, in a script called *Brahmi*.
N: For centuries after, nobody could read it. Until *eighteen thirty-seven*, when a British scholar, *James Prinsep*, cracked it, and Ashoka suddenly spoke again.
N: One of his pillars, at Sarnath, was topped with four lions standing back to back. You know it. It's on every Indian banknote and passport: India's *national emblem*.
N: And the wheel beneath the lions, the *Ashoka Chakra*, sits in the middle of India's flag.
TRAVELLER: [looking at the flag, impressed] Two thousand years later. Still on the flag.
N: But empires built by one great man rarely outlive him for long.
N: About fifty years after Ashoka died, the last Maurya emperor was killed by his own general, during a military parade. The empire broke apart. *Shatter*.
N: And into the north, through the mountain passes, came new arrivals once again.

# CHAPTER 5: THE GOLDEN AGE

N: For the next few centuries, India was ruled by many kingdoms at once. And in many ways, it got richer.
N: The *Kushans*, who came from Central Asia, built an empire across the north. Their king *Kanishka* became a great patron of Buddhism. *Absorb*.
N: In the south, ships sailed with the monsoon winds to Egypt and Rome, loaded with pepper, cotton cloth and gems.
N: So much Roman gold flowed to India that the Roman writer *Pliny* complained about it. He grumbled that India drained Rome of *fifty million sesterces* every year.
TRAVELLER: [checking a coin] The Golden Bird. Already collecting gold.
N: Then, around *three hundred twenty CE*, a new family united the north again: the *Guptas*. *Stitch*.
N: The Gupta age is often called India's *classical age*. And the reason is not its battles. It's its ideas.
N: The poet *Kalidasa* wrote plays and poems still performed today.
N: The mathematician *Aryabhata*, at just twenty-three, wrote that the Earth spins on its own axis... about a thousand years before Copernicus.
N: He worked out *pi* to four decimal places: three point one four one six.
N: And Indian mathematicians were developing the number system you use every day: nine digits, plus a place for *nothing*.
N: Just over a century later, the mathematician *Brahmagupta* wrote the rules for *zero* as a number in its own right. Add, subtract, multiply.
N: These numerals travelled to the Arab world and then to Europe. Which is why we call them... *Arabic numerals*.

[SKIT: a rooftop observatory at night, Aryabhata pointing at the stars, a royal official holding a lamp]
OFFICIAL: [confused] So the sky turns around the Earth every night?
ARYABHATA: No. The Earth turns. The sky stays still.
OFFICIAL: [looking down at his feet] Then why am I not dizzy?
ARYABHATA: [patiently] I'll explain tomorrow. If the Earth is still here.

N: Students came from across Asia to study at *Nalanda*, one of the world's earliest great universities, with thousands of students and a library so big, it filled several buildings.
N: Even the iron got better. The *Iron Pillar of Delhi*, made in the Gupta age, has stood in the open for about *sixteen hundred years*... and has barely rusted.
TRAVELLER: [knocking on the pillar] My bicycle rusted in one monsoon.
N: But in the five hundreds, raiders called the *Huna* swept in from Central Asia. The Gupta Empire cracked. *Shatter*. Again.
N: In the south, though, the story was just getting started.
N: The *Chola* kings of Tamil Nadu built one of the greatest navies in Asian history. *Rajaraja the First* built the vast *Brihadeeswarar Temple* at Thanjavur, finished around *ten ten*. Its tower is about sixty-six metres tall.
N: His son, *Rajendra*, sent his fleet across the Bay of Bengal to attack the mighty *Srivijaya* kingdom in Sumatra and Malaya.
TRAVELLER: [looking at a map] An Indian navy. Raiding Southeast Asia. A thousand years ago.
N: But in the north, things were about to change. A powerful sultan had just learned how rich India's temples were.

# CHAPTER 6: THE SULTANS

N: His name was *Mahmud of Ghazni*, from today's Afghanistan. Starting around *the year one thousand*, he raided north India again and again. Around *seventeen times*.
N: He came for gold. His raiders looted cities and temples, including the famous temple of *Somnath* in Gujarat, in *ten twenty-six*. Then he went home.
N: Mahmud didn't stay to rule. But about a hundred and fifty years later, another invader did: *Muhammad of Ghor*.
N: In *eleven ninety-one*, at *Tarain*, near Delhi, he met the Rajput king *Prithviraj Chauhan*. And Prithviraj won. Ghori was wounded and fled.
N: A year later, Ghori came back with a bigger army. Same battlefield. This time, Prithviraj lost.
TRAVELLER: [wincing] Rule one of history: if you beat someone, make sure they don't come back next year.
N: In *twelve oh six*, Ghori's general *Qutb ud-Din Aibak* became the first ruler of the *Delhi Sultanate*. He began the tallest brick minaret in the world: the *Qutb Minar*.
N: Around the same time, according to later chronicles, raiders led by *Bakhtiyar Khalji* attacked *Nalanda*, and the great university was ruined.
N: For the next three hundred years, five different dynasties would rule from Delhi.
N: One sultan, *Alauddin Khalji*, beat back several *Mongol* invasions. Unlike China, Persia and Russia, India was never conquered by the Mongols.
N: He also tried something very modern: *price controls*. He fixed the price of grain, horses, cloth, even slaves in the markets of Delhi, and punished cheating shopkeepers harshly.
TRAVELLER: [taking notes] The first price regulator. Strict. Very, very strict.
N: Then came the strangest sultan of all: *Muhammad bin Tughlaq*. Clever, well-read... and full of big ideas.
N: In *thirteen twenty-seven*, he decided to move the capital from Delhi to *Daulatabad*, about a thousand kilometres south. And he ordered much of Delhi's population to move with him.

[SKIT: a royal court in Delhi, Tughlaq on his throne, a worried courtier holding a map]
TUGHLAQ: [cheerful] Great news! We are moving the capital. Everybody pack.
COURTIER: Everybody, Your Majesty?
TUGHLAQ: Everybody.
COURTIER: [reading the map] It's... forty days' walk.
TUGHLAQ: Then start walking.
TRAVELLER: [whispering] A few years later, he moves everyone back.

N: He also issued *copper and brass coins* and declared them worth the same as silver.
N: Within months, people were making their own coins at home. Huge piles of fake money. He had to buy them all back with real silver.
TRAVELLER: [shaking his head] Great idea. Five hundred years too early.
N: In *thirteen ninety-eight*, the Central Asian conqueror *Timur* sacked Delhi. The city took a century to recover. *Shatter*.
N: Meanwhile, in the south, a powerful Hindu empire was rising: *Vijayanagara*, with its capital at *Hampi*. Foreign visitors said it was as big as Rome, and full of diamonds and horses.
N: But here's the thing about the Sultanate years. Something new was also being *made*.
N: Persian and Indian builders mixed their styles in arches, domes and minarets. The poet *Amir Khusrau* wrote in a mix of Persian and the local speech, and is traditionally credited with shaping *qawwali* music.
N: Out of the bazaars and army camps, a whole new language grew: what would become *Urdu*, and close cousin to *Hindi*.
N: *Absorb*. Again.
TRAVELLER: [smiling] India doesn't just get invaded. It gets... remixed.
N: In *fifteen twenty-six*, a young prince from Central Asia rode down from Kabul. He had lost his homeland more than once. He was looking for a new one.
N: His great-great-great-grandfather was Timur. And on his mother's side, he was descended from *Genghis Khan*.

# CHAPTER 7: THE MUGHALS RISE

N: His name was *Babur*. And at *Panipat*, north of Delhi, he faced the Sultan *Ibrahim Lodi*, who had a far bigger army and around a hundred elephants.
N: But Babur brought something new to India: *cannons* and *guns*. The elephants panicked. In a single day, the Sultanate was gone.
N: Babur founded the *Mughal Empire*. Stitch, again.
N: He also wrote a remarkable memoir, the *Baburnama*. And honestly? He wasn't that impressed with India.
N: He complained there were no good horses, no good grapes, and no good *melons*.

[SKIT: Babur's camp outside Delhi, Babur writing in a diary, a servant offering a plate of fruit]
SERVANT: [proudly] Your Majesty, the finest mangoes in Hindustan!
BABUR: [sniffing] No melons?
SERVANT: Mangoes, Majesty. Everyone loves them.
BABUR: [writing gloomily] Day four hundred. Still no melons.

N: Babur ruled for only four years. His son *Humayun* lost the whole empire to an Afghan general, *Sher Shah Suri*.
N: Sher Shah ruled for just five years, but he was brilliant. He improved the great road from Bengal to the northwest, later called the *Grand Trunk Road*, with rest houses and wells along the way.
N: And he issued a new silver coin. He called it the *rupiya*.
TRAVELLER: [holding up a coin] The rupee. Started by the guy who kicked the Mughals out.
N: Humayun came back fifteen years later and took Delhi again. A few months later, he slipped on the stairs of his library and died.
N: His son was thirteen years old. His name was *Akbar*.
N: In *fifteen fifty-six*, at a second battle at Panipat, Akbar's army defeated the general *Hemu*. And over the next fifty years, Akbar built one of the richest empires on Earth.
N: Akbar's secret weapon was not cannons. It was *including* people.
N: He made alliances with the Rajput kingdoms, many through marriage, and gave Rajput princes high posts in his army.
N: He scrapped the *jizya*, a tax paid by non-Muslims. And his finance minister, *Raja Todar Mal*, built a careful system for measuring land and setting taxes.
N: Akbar could barely read. Yet he collected a huge library, and had books read aloud to him every day.
N: At his new city, *Fatehpur Sikri*, he built a hall where Muslim, Hindu, Jain, Zoroastrian and Christian scholars debated religion in front of him.
TRAVELLER: [whispering] The debates got so heated, the Emperor had to play referee.
N: Not everyone accepted Akbar. The Rajput ruler of Mewar, *Maharana Pratap*, refused to submit, and fought Akbar's army at *Haldighati* in *fifteen seventy-six*. He kept resisting from the hills for the rest of his life.
N: Still, by the time Akbar died in *sixteen oh five*, the Mughal Empire was enormous. And it was only getting richer.
N: Remember the hook? That *quarter of the world's economy*? That was the India his grandson would rule.
TRAVELLER: [checking his watch, excited] Now we're getting to the gold.

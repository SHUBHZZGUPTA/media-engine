# India — pilot script v1 (hook + chapter 1)

Format: `N:` narrator · `TRAVELLER:` our time-traveller mascot · other speakers are skit characters. `*word*` = keyword caption. `[tag]` = delivery. ~980 words ≈ 6 min.
Numbers marked ‡ are historians' estimates (Maddison) and are framed as estimates on screen. Theories about the Indus decline are framed as theories.

---

# HOOK

N: Three hundred years ago, one place on Earth made about *a quarter* of everything the whole world produced.
N: Not Britain. Not China. Not America.
N: *India.*‡
N: Spices, cotton, steel, diamonds. Ships came from the other side of the planet just to buy what India made.
TRAVELLER: [checking his pocket watch, impressed] Richest shop on the planet. And it was always open.
N: India was so rich, people had a name for it: *Sone ki Chidiya*. The *Golden Bird*.
TRAVELLER: [nervous] And when you're a golden bird... everyone wants to catch you.
N: Then, in less than two hundred and fifty years... that quarter shrank to about *four percent*.‡
TRAVELLER: [winces] Ah. Someone forgot to lock the shop.
N: And here's the strange part. This wasn't the first time. India has been *rich*... and *robbed*... and *rebuilt*... again and again, for five thousand years.
N: Over that time, this land was ruled by city-builders nobody can name, an emperor who gave up war after one terrible battle, sultans, Mughals, a *company*... and finally, its own people.
TRAVELLER: [nervous] A company. I'll explain later. You will not believe it.
N: Because if you look closely, India keeps living *the same story*.
N: First, someone *stitches* the land together. Then it *shines*, the richest place on Earth. Then it *shatters* into pieces. Outsiders march in to take it...
N: ...and then something strange happens. India *absorbs* them. The invaders end up eating its food, speaking its languages, and building its monuments.
N: Stitch. Shine. Shatter. Absorb. *Again and again.*
N: So how did one land become the richest place in the world, lose it all, and start climbing back?
TRAVELLER: [winding his watch] Hold on to something.
N: Let's go back to the very beginning.

# CHAPTER 1: THE CITY NOBODY CAN READ

N: About forty-five hundred years ago, while Egypt was building its pyramids, a civilisation was growing along the *Indus River*, in what is today *Pakistan and northwest India*.
N: We call it the *Indus Valley Civilisation*. And it was *huge*. It covered more land than Egypt and Mesopotamia *put together*.
N: Its biggest cities, *Harappa* and *Mohenjo-daro*, were home to tens of thousands of people.
N: And these cities were... weirdly *modern*.
N: Streets ran in neat, straight lines. Houses were built from bricks of the *same size*, everywhere, for hundreds of years.
N: Many houses had their own *bathroom*. Wastewater flowed into *covered drains* under the street.
TRAVELLER: [looking down at a drain, stunned] Four and a half thousand years ago. Underground drainage.
TRAVELLER: [to camera] Some cities today are still working on this.

N: In the middle of Mohenjo-daro sat something archaeologists call the *Great Bath*. A brick pool, sealed with tar so it wouldn't leak.
N: Nobody knows exactly what it was for. Maybe ceremonies. Maybe a very fancy swimming pool.

[SKIT: Mohenjo-daro street, a city inspector with a measuring rod, a nervous brick-maker holding a slightly bigger brick]
INSPECTOR: [holding up the brick] What is this?
BRICKMAKER: A brick, sir. A *nice* brick.
INSPECTOR: It's *bigger*. One to two to four. Every brick. Every city. Every time.
BRICKMAKER: [quietly] I was trying to be creative.
INSPECTOR: [hands it back] Be creative with *pots*.

N: They even used *standard weights* for trading, so nobody got cheated at the market.
N: And they traded far. Indus seals have been found all the way in *Mesopotamia*, in today's Iraq.
N: But here's the mystery.
N: These people wrote. On seals, on pots, on tags. Hundreds of different signs.
N: And *nobody* can read them. Not one word. To this day.
TRAVELLER: [squinting at a seal with a unicorn-like animal] Fish. Jar. Unicorn. [shrugs] Either a king's name or a grocery list.
N: So we don't know what they called themselves. We don't know their kings, their gods, or their stories.
N: Oddly, archaeologists haven't found grand palaces or giant royal tombs either. Just well-planned cities, built for ordinary people.

N: Then, around *nineteen hundred BCE*, the great cities slowly emptied.
N: Why? Nobody knows for sure. Historians suspect a mix: rivers changing course, a river drying up, and the *monsoon* growing weaker for years.
N: People drifted east and south into smaller villages. The drains filled with mud. The cities were *forgotten*.
N: For almost four thousand years.

N: And how did the world rediscover them? Through a *railway*.
N: In the eighteen-fifties, British engineers were building a railway line in Punjab. They needed lots of bricks.
N: And near a village called *Harappa*, they found a huge pile of old, perfect bricks just lying in the ground.

[SKIT: 1850s railway site, a British engineer with a clipboard, a foreman pointing at a mound of bricks]
FOREMAN: Sir, there's an old ruin full of bricks over there.
ENGINEER: [delighted] Free bricks? Splendid. Old ruins never matter.
TRAVELLER: [face in his hands] They matter. They really, *really* matter.

N: Bricks from a four-thousand-year-old city ended up under the tracks.
N: Only in the *nineteen-twenties* did archaeologists realise what they were digging up: one of the *oldest civilisations* on Earth.
N: So that was India's first *shine*. Rich, organised, connected to the world.
N: And then, its first *shatter*. Its cities fell silent.
TRAVELLER: [making a note] Loop step one. Check.
N: But the land didn't stay empty for long. Over the mountains in the northwest, new people were coming. They brought horses, chariots...
N: ...and a collection of hymns so sacred, it was passed down *word for word*, by memory alone, for more than three thousand years.
TRAVELLER: [watch ticking, leaning in] Now *this* one, people still argue about.

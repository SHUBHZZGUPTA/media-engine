# CHAPTER 1: WHY GOLD?
N: Before there was a note, there was a problem. You have *chickens.* You want a *goat.* The owner *hates chickens.* Economists call this the *double coincidence of wants.* Everyone else calls it a *wasted afternoon.*
N: So people tried *shells,* *cattle,* *metal...* and one material kept winning. *Gold.*
N: It doesn't *rust.* It doesn't *rot.* It's *rare,* but not too rare. You can *melt* it, *split* it and *stamp* it. And *everyone, everywhere,* already wanted it.
N: More than *twenty-five centuries* ago, in *Lydia,* in what is now western Turkey, lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, were stamped with a *lion's head.* They're among the *earliest coins* we know of. No more weighing metal at every sale. You could just *count.*
N: But gold had one big problem. It's *heavy.* Try buying a house with a *backpack full of coins.* So *paper* slipped in through the *back door.*
GOLDIE: [smug] Told you paper *couldn't be trusted.*
BILL: [offended] I'm *literally right here.*

# CHAPTER 2: PAPER ARRIVES
N: About a *thousand years* ago, in *Sichuan,* China, the local coins were made of *iron.* Heavy, and not worth much. Carrying your savings meant carrying a *small anvil.*
N: So merchants left their coins with *trusted shops* and carried *paper receipts* instead, called *jiaozi.* When some of those shops *went bust,* the *Song government* took over. In *1024,* it issued what's often called the *world's first government paper money.*
N: Then it discovered the oldest temptation in money. If paper works... why not *print a little more?* *More notes.* *Higher prices.*
BILL: [sheepish] In my defence, they *printed me.*
N: That's the *first mirage* in this story: paper only works while people *believe* what's printed on it.
N: Europe caught on later. From *1694,* *Bank of England* notes promised to pay the bearer, and for much of the next two centuries, you really could *swap them for gold.* Paper was the *ticket.* Gold sat in the *vault.* That's a *gold standard.*
N: Sounds perfect. So why did every country eventually *walk away* from it?

# CHAPTER 3: THE GOLD TRAP
N: Here's the catch. Money could only grow as fast as the *gold pile.* And in a *panic,* everyone wants their gold back *at the same moment.* There's *never enough* for everyone at once.
N: So in wars and crises, countries kept *suspending the promise.* Britain broke the link for good in *1931.*
N: America went further. On *April 5th, 1933,* in the depths of the Great Depression, President Franklin Roosevelt signed *Executive Order 6102.* Americans had to *hand in* most of their gold coins, bars and gold certificates by *May 1st,* paid at the official price of *20 dollars 67 cents* an ounce.
N: Hoard it, and you risked a *10,000-dollar fine,* *ten years in prison,* or both. Imagine explaining that one at a dinner party.
N: A year later, the official price jumped to *35 dollars.* Same gold. But each dollar now stood for about *41 percent less* of it. Our hundred-dollar bill? Its claim fell from *almost five ounces* to *under three.*
GOLDIE: [delighted] Same me. *Bigger price tag.*
BILL: [grumbling] *Smaller me.*
N: And Americans couldn't legally own gold bars again until the *last day of 1974.* But here's the *loophole.* *Ordinary people* were locked out of gold. *Foreign governments* weren't. And that loophole is what eventually *broke the whole system.*

# CHAPTER 4: AS GOOD AS GOLD
N: *July 1944.* The Second World War is still raging, and delegates from *44 countries* check into a hotel in *Bretton Woods,* New Hampshire. Not for a holiday.
N: The plan: other currencies *peg to the dollar,* and the dollar *pegs to gold* at *35 dollars* an ounce, swappable by foreign governments. Why the dollar? Because in the early post-war years, America held roughly *three quarters* of the world's official gold.
N: Suddenly the dollar was *as good as gold,* and better in one way. You could *earn interest* on dollars. Gold just sits there, *looking expensive.*
GOLDIE: [offended] I prefer *"timeless."*
N: But there was a *trap* built in. To give the world enough dollars, America had to keep *sending them abroad.* The more dollars piled up overseas, the *less American gold could cover them.* The economist Robert Triffin warned about this in *1959.* It's now called the *Triffin dilemma.*
N: *France* noticed. In *January 1965,* it announced it would swap at least *300 million* of its dollars for American gold. Its finance minister, Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, had a name for America's position: an *"exorbitant privilege."*
N: America plugged the leaks with a shared *gold pool* in London and emergency deals between central banks. In *March 1968,* the gold pool *collapsed.* The dam was *cracking.*

# CHAPTER 5: THE SUNDAY IT CHANGED
N: *August 1971.* President Richard Nixon takes his economic team to *Camp David* for the weekend. And on *Sunday night, August 15th,* he goes on *television.*
N: A *90-day freeze* on wages and prices. A *10 percent surcharge* on imports. And the big one: the *gold window was closed.* Foreign governments could *no longer swap* their dollars for American gold.
N: Nixon called it *temporary.*
BILL: [hopeful, then unsure] So... *temporary,* right?
N: It *never came back.* By *1973,* the major currencies were *floating,* priced by markets instead of metal. Our hundred-dollar bill was now backed by *no gold at all.*
N: Which leaves a *puzzle.* The dollar lost its last link to gold more than *fifty years* ago. You'd expect the world to *dump it.* Instead, it became *even more powerful.* *Why?*

# CHAPTER 6: WHY THE WORLD STILL RUNS ON DOLLARS
N: Part of it is *oil.* In *1974,* the US and Saudi Arabia reached an *informal understanding:* Saudi oil money flowed into *US government bonds,* and America offered *military support.* No treaty ever forced oil into dollars, but oil has mostly been *priced in dollars* ever since.
N: The bigger reason is a *network effect.* It's why your whole family ends up on the *same messaging app,* including the uncle who only sends *good-morning memes.* If every bank, exporter and central bank already uses dollars, the easiest thing for you to do is *use dollars too.*
N: The numbers are wild. In *April 2025,* the dollar was on one side of about *89 percent* of all currency trades, in a market moving roughly *9.6 trillion dollars a day.* Central banks keep about *57 percent* of their foreign reserves in dollars. The euro, in second place, sits around *21.*
N: But gold *never really left.* Central banks bought more than a *thousand tonnes* of it in each of *2022, 2023 and 2024,* and another *863 tonnes* in *2025.*

# CHAPTER 7: SO WHAT IS YOUR MONEY, REALLY?
N: So why not just go *back to gold?* Here's the problem. *All the gold ever mined,* about *222,600 tonnes,* would fit in a *single cube* roughly *22.6 metres* on each side.
N: Tie the world's money to that cube, and money can only grow *as fast as miners can dig.* In a crisis, central banks' *hands are tied.* Many economists, including former Fed chair *Ben Bernanke,* argue that's part of what made the *Great Depression* so brutal: the *longer* a country clung to gold, the *deeper it sank.*
N: So, back to the question we started with. What is your money *backed by?* Read that *promise* on the note again. Today, the Bank of England says its notes can only be exchanged for... *other Bank of England notes.*
N: What really stands behind it is *trust.* Trust that the central bank keeps *prices stable.* Trust in a government that *collects taxes* in that currency. And trust that *everyone else will still accept it* tomorrow.
N: Gold was a promise you could *hold.* Paper is a promise you have to *believe.* And the moment enough people *stop believing...* that's when the *mirage disappears.*

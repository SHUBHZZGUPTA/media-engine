# CHAPTER 1: WHY GOLD?
N: Trading without money is a nightmare. You have chickens. You want a goat. The goat owner hates chickens. Economists call this the double coincidence of wants. Everyone else calls it a wasted afternoon.
N: So people tried shells, cattle, metal... and one material kept winning. Gold.
N: It doesn't rust. It doesn't rot. It's rare, but not too rare. You can melt it, split it and stamp it. And everyone, everywhere, already wanted it.
N: Around 600 BC, in Lydia, in what is now western Turkey, someone stamped lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, with an official mark. They're among the earliest coins we know of. No more weighing metal at every sale. You could just count.
N: But gold had one big problem. It's heavy. Try buying a house with a backpack full of coins. And that problem let paper in through the back door.

# CHAPTER 2: PAPER ARRIVES
N: About a thousand years ago, in Chengdu, in China's Sichuan region, the local coins were made of iron. Heavy, and not worth much. Carrying your savings meant carrying a small anvil.
N: So merchants left their coins with trusted businesses and carried paper notes instead, called jiaozi. When some of those businesses went bust, the Song government took over in 1023, and in 1024 it issued what's often called the world's first government paper money.
N: Then it discovered the oldest temptation in money. If paper works... why not print a little more? Extra notes kept coming. Prices kept rising.
BILL: [sheepish] In my defence... they printed me.
N: That's the first mirage in this story. Paper only works while people believe what's printed on it.
N: Europe caught on later. From 1694, Bank of England notes promised to pay the bearer, and for much of the time until 1931, the Bank could pay out in gold. Paper was the ticket. Gold sat in the vault. That's a gold standard.

# CHAPTER 3: THE GOLD TRAP
N: In calm times, it worked. But here's the catch. Money could only grow as fast as the gold pile. And in a panic, everyone wants their gold back at the same moment. There's never enough for everyone at once.
N: So in emergencies, especially wars, countries kept suspending the link. Britain cut it for good in 1931.
N: America went further. On April 5th, 1933, in the depths of the Great Depression, President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 6102. Americans had to hand most of their gold coins, bars and gold certificates to the banking system by May 1st, at 20 dollars 67 cents an ounce. Hoard it, and you risked a 10,000-dollar fine, up to ten years in prison, or both.
N: Imagine explaining that one at a dinner party.
N: A year later, the official price jumped to 35 dollars an ounce. Same gold. But each dollar now stood for about 41 percent less of it. And Americans couldn't legally own gold bars again until the last day of 1974.
N: So ordinary people were locked out of gold. But foreign governments weren't. And that loophole is what eventually broke the whole system.

# CHAPTER 4: THE DOLLAR BECOMES THE WORLD'S GOLD
N: July 1944. The Second World War is still raging, and delegates from 44 countries check into a hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. Not for a holiday.
N: The plan: other currencies peg to the dollar, and the dollar pegs to gold, at 35 dollars an ounce, swappable by foreign governments. Why the dollar? Because at the start, America held about three quarters of the world's official gold.
N: Suddenly the dollar was as good as gold, and better in one way. You could invest dollars. Gold just sits there, looking expensive.
N: But there was a trap built in. To give the world enough dollars, America had to keep sending them abroad. And the more dollars piled up overseas, the less the gold in American vaults could cover them. Economists call this the Triffin dilemma.
N: France noticed. In January 1965, it announced it would swap at least 300 million of its dollars for American gold. Its finance minister, Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, called America's position an "exorbitant privilege."
N: America plugged the leaks with a shared gold pool in London and emergency swaps between central banks. In 1968, the gold pool collapsed.

# CHAPTER 5: THE SUNDAY IT CHANGED
N: By August 1971, the dam was cracking. President Richard Nixon took his economic team to Camp David for the weekend. And on Sunday, August 15th, he went on television.
N: A 90-day freeze on wages and prices. A 10 percent surcharge on imports. And the big one: the gold window was closed. Foreign governments could no longer swap their dollars for American gold.
N: Nixon pitched it as a pause, with gold to return once the system was fixed.
BILL: [hopeful, then unsure] So... temporary, right?
N: It never came back. By 1973, the major currencies were floating, priced by markets instead of metal.
N: Which leaves a puzzle. The dollar lost its last link to gold more than fifty years ago. You'd expect the world to dump it. Instead, it became even more central. Why?

# CHAPTER 6: WHY THE WORLD STILL RUNS ON DOLLARS
N: Part of it is oil. In 1974, the US and Saudi Arabia reached an informal understanding. Saudi oil money flowed into US government bonds, and America offered military support. No formal treaty ever forced oil into dollars, but oil has mostly traded in dollars ever since.
N: The bigger reason is a network effect. It's why your whole family ends up on the same messaging app, including the relative who only sends good-morning memes. If every bank, every exporter and every central bank already uses dollars, the easiest thing for you to do... is use dollars too.
N: The numbers are wild. In April 2025, the dollar was on one side of about 89 percent of all currency trades, in a market moving roughly 9.6 trillion dollars a day. Central banks keep about 57 percent of their known foreign-currency reserves in dollars. The euro, in second place, sits around 20.
N: Behind that sits a huge, easy-to-trade market for US government bonds. When the world gets scared, that's where a lot of its money has traditionally run.
N: But gold never really left. Central banks bought more than 1,000 tonnes of it in each of 2022, 2023 and 2024, and another 863 tonnes in 2025.

# CHAPTER 7: SO WHAT IS YOUR MONEY, REALLY?
N: So why not just go back to gold? Here's the problem. All the gold ever mined, about 222,600 tonnes, would fit in a single cube roughly 22.6 metres on each side.
N: Tie the world's money to that cube, and money can only grow as fast as miners can dig. In a crisis, central banks' hands are tied. Many economists argue that's part of what made the Great Depression so brutal.
N: So, back to the question we started with. What is your money backed by? Read that British note again. Today, the Bank of England says its promise mostly means one thing: you can swap the note... for another Bank of England note.
N: What really stands behind it is trust. Trust that the central bank keeps prices stable. Trust in a government that collects taxes in that currency. And trust that everyone else will still accept it tomorrow.
N: Gold was a promise you could hold. Paper is a promise you have to believe. And the moment enough people stop believing... that's when the mirage disappears.

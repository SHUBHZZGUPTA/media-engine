# COLD OPEN
N: Your money lost its gold in 1971. So why hasn't it died? What is it actually backed by?
N: Look at the small print on a British pound, or an Indian rupee, and you'll find the same promise: "I promise to pay the bearer." Pay you what?
N: In early 1933, a hundred-dollar bill was a claim on almost five ounces of gold. By the 1960s, less than three. Today, spend it on gold, and you'd get about a fortieth of an ounce.
N: Somewhere between those numbers is one Sunday night in 1971, when that promise quietly changed... and never changed back.
N: So let's follow one banknote through twenty-six centuries of money, and find out what's really standing behind yours.

# CHAPTER 1: WHY GOLD?
N: Before there was a note, there was a problem. You have chickens. You want a goat. The goat owner hates chickens. Economists call this the double coincidence of wants. Everyone else calls it a wasted afternoon.
N: So people tried shells, cattle, metal... and one material kept winning. Gold.
N: It doesn't rust. It doesn't rot. It's rare, but not too rare. You can melt it, split it and stamp it. And everyone, everywhere, already wanted it.
N: More than twenty-five centuries ago, in Lydia, in what is now western Turkey, lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, were stamped with a lion's head. They're among the earliest coins we know of. No more weighing metal at every sale. You could just count.
N: But gold had one big problem. It's heavy. Try buying a house with a backpack full of coins. And that problem let paper in through the back door.

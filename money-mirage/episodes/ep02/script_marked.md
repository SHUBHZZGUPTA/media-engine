# CHAPTER 1: THE PROMISE
N: Imagine your *rupee,* your *pound* or your *dollar* changed value against its neighbours *every single day.* For a factory selling across Europe in the *1980s,* that was normal. Profits could vanish because a currency *wobbled* overnight.
N: So in *1979,* a group of European countries built a *fence.* It was called the *Exchange Rate Mechanism,* or *ERM.* Each currency could drift, but only *inside a band.* Hit the edge of the band, and the central bank had to *step in* and defend it.
N: Britain stayed out for over a decade. Then, on *October 8th, 1990,* it *jumped in.* The rate: one pound for *2.95 Deutschmarks.* The band: *six percent* either way. The floor: *2.778.* Below that, the Bank of England had to *buy pounds,* whatever it cost. The man who signed Britain up was the finance minister, Chancellor *John Major.* The same day, rates were cut from *15 to 14 percent.*
N: Why tie yourself to *Germany?* Because Britain had a *problem.* In *1990,* inflation hit *10.9 percent.* Germany's central bank, the *Bundesbank,* was the toughest inflation fighter in the world. Peg your money to the *Deutschmark,* and you *borrow its reputation.*
N: It's like a student who can't stop spending, handing his card to the *strictest friend he has.* It works... as long as the friend's plans *match yours.*

# CHAPTER 2: THE GERMAN PROBLEM
N: Here's the catch. Days before Britain joined, *East and West Germany* reunited. Rebuilding the East cost *enormous* sums, and German money started growing *too fast.* By *July 1992,* German money supply was running around *9 percent* against a target of *3.5 to 5.5.*
N: So the Bundesbank did what it always did. It *raised rates.* The discount rate went to *8.75 percent.* The Lombard rate sat at *9.75.* For Germany, that made sense.
N: For *Britain,* it was a *disaster.* Britain was in a *recession.* Homeowners were drowning. Lenders repossessed about *75,000 homes* in *1991,* and nearly *69,000* more in *1992.* By the end of 1992, somewhere between *one and two million* households owed more than their homes were worth.
N: And here's the cruel part. The peg was *working.* By the summer of *1992,* British inflation was down to *3.6 percent.* The medicine had done its job. But Britain couldn't stop *taking* it.
N: Britain *needed* cheaper money. But with the pound pinned near its floor, it *couldn't cut rates* without the pound sinking through it. Its interest rates were now set, in effect, by a central bank in *Frankfurt,* worrying about *Berlin.*
N: That's the *mirage* in this story. A fixed exchange rate *looks* like a fact. It isn't. It's a *promise,* and it only holds while someone is *willing and able* to defend it.
BILL: [sheepish] So the price on me is just... *a promise?*

# CHAPTER 3: THE SPECULATOR
N: Across the Atlantic, someone was watching that promise *very closely.* *George Soros* ran the *Quantum Fund* in New York. Day to day, it was managed by *Stanley Druckenmiller,* and by most accounts, the pound trade was *his idea.*
N: Here's how you bet against a currency. You *borrow* pounds, and immediately *sell* them for Deutschmarks at around *2.78.* If the pound later falls to, say, *2.50,* you buy those pounds back *cheaper,* return what you borrowed, and *keep the difference.*
N: Now look at the *odds.* The pound was sitting near its *floor.* If Britain held the line, the pound could only rise *a little,* so the bet loses *a little.* If Britain *broke,* the pound could fall *a long way,* and the bet wins *big.*
N: *Heads,* you lose a little. *Tails,* you win a lot. Traders dream of a bet like that.
N: But wait. Isn't the Bank of England *unbeatable?* It can print *as many pounds* as it likes. Here's the problem. To *hold up* the pound, it has to *buy* pounds, and pay for them in *other* currencies, like Deutschmarks and dollars. Those come from its *reserves.* And the Bank of England can't print a *single Deutschmark.* Its pockets are *deep.* But they have a *bottom.*
N: And Soros had a hunch about *Germany.* Economic historian *Alain Naef* found that Soros met Bundesbank president *Helmut Schlesinger* in *Basel,* and came away convinced the *lira* and the *pound* would soon fall. Germany, he reckoned, would not *bend* to save them.

# CHAPTER 4: THE CRACKS
N: By late summer *1992,* the cracks were showing. John Major was now *Prime Minister.* In August, his new Chancellor, *Norman Lamont,* said Britain would do *whatever is necessary* to stay in. On *September 3rd,* the government announced it would borrow *10 billion ECU,* Europe's accounting currency, about *7 billion pounds,* to defend sterling.
N: Then *Italy* blinked. Over the weekend of *September 12th,* the *lira* was devalued by *7 percent.* In return, Germany *trimmed* its rates. Just *slightly.* Less than markets hoped.
N: Then came the *spark.* On *Tuesday, September 15th,* after the London markets closed, Schlesinger spoke to reporters from *Handelsblatt* and the *Wall Street Journal.* In substance: the weekend's deal should have been *broader,* and more pressure on currencies *couldn't be ruled out.*
N: By that evening, it was on the *news wires.* Traders read it one way: *the pound is next.* That day, the Bank of England had already sold about *788 million dollars* to prop up the pound. It closed at *2.7812.* Barely above the *floor.*
N: Druckenmiller wanted to *add* to the bet. According to Druckenmiller himself, and financial historian *Sebastian Mallaby,* Soros told him to *go for the jugular.* They didn't just bet the fund. They bet roughly *twice* the fund. By Soros's own estimate, the position was worth almost *ten billion dollars.*

# CHAPTER 5: BLACK WEDNESDAY
N: *Wednesday, September 16th, 1992.* By *8 a.m.,* the pound is at *2.7794* Deutschmarks. That's a hair above the *floor.* The Bank of England starts *buying.* By *9,* it has spent more than *a billion pounds.* The pound *doesn't move.* Help from Germany? According to Naef, the Bundesbank bought pounds for Britain *just once* that day: about *809 million pounds.*
N: At *11 a.m.,* the big gun. Interest rates go from *10 to 12 percent* in one move. The idea: make holding pounds so rewarding that sellers *stop selling.* They *keep selling.*
N: Lamont later said that once the rate rise failed, he told Prime Minister *John Major* the game was *up.* But Britain wasn't done.
N: At *2:15 p.m.,* it announces rates will rise again, to *15 percent.* Fifteen. For a country already in *recession.* Overnight cash in the money markets is quoted at *100 percent.* Everyone can see what's happening. A government *raising rates* this fast isn't *strong.* It's *desperate.*

# CHAPTER 6: WHO PAID THE BILL?
N: Freed from its floor, the pound *fell.* By the end of *1992,* it was around *2.45* Deutschmarks, roughly *17 percent* below the rate Britain had promised. Against the dollar, it slid from about *1.85* to about *1.52.*

# CHAPTER 7: THE TWIST
N: Now the *twist.* Without the peg, Britain could finally set its *own* rates. By *November,* they were down to *7 percent.* In *October 1992,* it adopted something new: an *inflation target,* a public goal of *1 to 4 percent.* And on *May 6th, 1997,* just days after winning power, Labour's new Chancellor, *Gordon Brown,* gave the Bank of England the power to set rates *itself.* Lamont says Brown phoned him the day before, to say Labour was taking *his* advice.
N: Many economists credit the *cheaper pound* and that new target with the recovery that followed. And Britain never joined the *euro.*
N: So was Soros the *villain?* He didn't create Britain's problem. He *spotted* it. A country had promised a price it *couldn't afford to keep,* and he bet that the promise would *break before the bank did.*

Life.
No sunlight. Freezing water. Crushing pressure. And still... there are animals living at the bottom of the trench.
Like the snailfish. A small, pale, almost see-through fish with a soft, jelly-like body.
Its body is made for the pressure. Its bones are soft, it has no swim bladder full of air, and special molecules in its cells help stop the pressure from bending its proteins out of shape.
In 2022, scientists filmed a snailfish swimming at eight thousand three hundred and thirty-six metres, in a trench just north of the Mariana, off Japan. The deepest fish ever caught on camera.
Below that depth, scientists think fish can't survive. But other animals can.
These are amphipods. Relatives of the tiny sand-hoppers you find on a beach. But in the deep trenches, some species grow far bigger than normal. Sometimes as long as your hand. Scientists call it deep-sea gigantism.
They crawl across the sea floor eating anything that sinks from above. Dead fish. Old leaves. Even scraps of wood.
And there are stranger things. Giant single-celled creatures called xenophyophores, each one just one cell, but as big as a lemon, have been found more than ten kilometres down in this trench.
Life, it turns out, will move in anywhere. Even here.
But the biggest secret of the Mariana Trench isn't living on the sea floor.
It's happening underneath it.
